\title{{\tt NuOscProbExact}: a fast, general-purpose code to compute\\
exact two-, three-, and four-flavor neutrino oscillation probabilities}

\author{Mauricio Bustamante\corref{author}}
\ead{mbustamante@nbi.ku.dk}
\cortext[author]{ORCID: \href{http://orcid.org/0000-0001-6923-0865}{0000-0001-6923-0865}.}
\address{Niels Bohr International Academy, Niels Bohr Institute,\\University of Copenhagen, DK-2100 Copenhagen, Denmark}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}

In neutrino oscillations, a neutrino created with one flavor can be later detected with a different flavor, with some probability.  In general, the probability is computed exactly by diagonalizing the Hamiltonian operator that describes the physical system and that drives the oscillations.  Here we use an alternative method developed by Ohlsson \& Snellman to compute exact oscillation probabilities, that bypasses diagonalization, and that produces expressions for the probabilities that are straightforward to implement.  The method employs expansions of quantum operators in terms of SU(2), SU(3), and SU(4) matrices.  We implement the method in the code {\tt NuOscProbExact}, which we make publicly available.  It can be applied to any closed system of two, three, or four neutrino flavors described by an arbitrary time-independent Hamiltonian.  This includes, but is not limited to, oscillations in vacuum, in matter of constant density, with non-standard matter interactions, and in a Lorentz-violating background.  It also treats matter of piece-wise constant density, including the density profile of Earth, by composing the exact solutions for consecutive slabs.

\end{abstract}

{\em Nature of problem:} \\
Computation of exact neutrino flavor oscillation probabilities in arbitrary physical systems described by time-independent Hamiltonians.  Applicable to closed systems of two, three, and four neutrino flavors, in vacuum, in matter of constant density, and in matter whose density varies along the trajectory, including the Earth. \\ \\
\smallskip
%%%%
{\em Solution method:}\\
Closed-form expansions of the time-evolution quantum operators using SU(2), SU(3), and SU(4) identities.  For two and three flavors the program is a lightweight implementation of the method of Refs.\ [1, 2].  Those references treat only those two cases; the four-flavor case is obtained instead from the spectral form of the evolution operator, which holds at any number of flavors and of which their expansion is one projection, and is the largest case for which the eigenvalues are available in closed form.  A varying density profile is cut into slabs of constant density, each solved exactly, and the operators composed in order. \\ \\
\smallskip
%%%%
{\em Additional comments, including restrictions and unusual features:}\\
The Hamiltonian must be Hermitian and time-independent, and the system closed: interactions, absorption, decay and other non-unitary terms are outside the method, and a code that integrates the density matrix should be used for them.  Beyond four flavors no closed form for the eigenvalues exists, and the construction no longer applies.  Near a level crossing, where the spectrum is nearly degenerate, forming the characteristic polynomial costs accuracy that a backward-stable eigensolver would keep.  Where the density varies continuously, as in the Earth, the profile is discretised and the answer is exact only for the slabs it is given, so the slab count is a precision setting like any other; it can be set directly or chosen by the routine from a requested tolerance.  Unusually for a code of this kind, no approximation is made in the probability itself: the result is exact to round-off for the Hamiltonian supplied, at any number of flavors up to four and for any Hermitian Hamiltonian, including non-standard interactions and Lorentz-violating terms. \\ \\

\section{Introduction}\label{Section_Introduction}

Neutrinos are created and detected in weak interactions as flavor states --- $\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$, $\nu_\tau$ --- but they propagate as superpositions of propagation states --- in vacuum, these are the mass eigenstates $\nu_1$, $\nu_2$, $\nu_3$.  Because the superposition evolves with time, a neutrino created with a certain flavor has a non-zero probability of being detected later with a different flavor\ \cite{Pontecorvo:1967fh, Barger:1980hs, Bilenky:2004sn, Fantini:2018itu}.  The observation of oscillations in solar, atmospheric, reactor, and accelerator neutrinos has led to the momentous discovery of neutrino mass and of flavor mixing in leptons\ \cite{Kajita:2016cak, McDonald:2016ixn}.

Computing the probabilities of flavor transition is integral to studying oscillations.  Computing them exactly typically involves diagonalizing the Hamiltonian operator that drives the time-evolution of neutrinos.  But, because the expressions involved are often complex, it is notoriously hard to produce exact analytical expressions for the probabilities that also provide physical insight.  The case of oscillations in vacuum is an exception\ \cite{Kayser:1981ye, Giunti:2007ry, Tanabashi:2018oca}.  Beyond that, there is a large body of work dedicated to deriving exact probabilities for different scenarios; see, \eg, \Refs\ \cite{Barger:1980tf, Petcov:1987zj, Zaglauer:1988gz, TorrenteLujan:1995qi, Balantekin:1997jp, Coleman:1998ti, Gago:2001xg, Kimura:2002wd, Harrison:2003fi, Kostelecky:2003xn, Kostelecky:2003cr, GonzalezGarcia:2004wg, Blennow:2004qd, Meloni:2009ia, Ando:2009ts, Abe:2014wla, Flores:2015mah, Popov:2018seq}.  Yet, though some of these expressions are superficially elegant, they are seldom used due to their underlying complexity, particularly in the case of oscillations amongst three neutrino flavors.

More often, carefully selected perturbative expansions and approximations are employed to cast the probabilities in forms that are amenable to physical interpretation.  Many such approximate expressions exist in the literature\ \cite{Gago:2002na, Blennow:2008eb, Diaz:2009qk, Meloni:2009ia, Chaves:2018sih}, especially for oscillations in matter\ \cite{Petcov:1986qg, Hirota:1998wv, Cervera:2000kp, Freund:2001pn, Akhmedov:2004ny, Friedland:2006pi, Ioannisian:2008ve, Supanitsky:2008eq, Minakata:2015gra, Flores:2015mah, Li:2016pzm, Denton:2016wmg, Parke:2019vbs} with precisions that reach the per-cent level.  Unfortunately, there is no systematic way to produce these useful expressions, since they are tailored to specific Hamiltonians (however, see, \Ref\ \cite{Denton:2018fex}), their derivation is not trivial, or their application is limited to specific ranges of values of a perturbative parameter.

Hence, the best course of action in cases where we seek high precision in the computation of probabilities is simply to compute them exactly, often numerically.  Figure \ref{fig:validation} shows what ``exactly'' buys and what it costs nothing: the probabilities returned by the closed forms below agree, to double-precision round-off, with those obtained by exponentiating the same Hamiltonian numerically, at two, three, and four flavors alike.  This is a common strategy to explore non-standard oscillation scenarios, \ie, arbitrary Hamiltonians, for which analytic solutions are in general unavailable.  For instance, this is done when scanning a parameter space without knowing {\it a priori} our region of interest, or approximate expressions of the probabilities that are valid inside that region.   

Here, in lieu of diagonalizing the Hamiltonian, we use an alternative method to compute exact oscillation probabilities, developed for two and three flavors by Ohlsson \& Snellman (hereafter, OS) in \Refs\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb, Ohlsson:1999um, Ohlsson:2000zz, Ohlsson:2001et}, and resting, for any number of flavors, on a spectral identity that is older than the oscillation problem.  We provide a numerical implementation for systems of two, three, and four neutrino flavors.  The method relies on expanding the quantum operators that drive the time-evolution of neutrinos in terms of SU(2), SU(3), and SU(4) matrices\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb, Ohlsson:1999um, Ohlsson:2000zz, Ohlsson:2001et}.  It has two assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item 
  The system must be closed, \ie, it must conserve the number of neutrinos summed over all flavors
 \item
  The Hamiltonian must be time-independent along the path, although a Hamiltonian that varies with time or position can often be treated by concatenating constant pieces, or approximated by doing so; see Section \ref{section:prob_3nu_matter}
\end{enumerate}
Both conditions are satisfied in many physical scenarios studied in the literature, \eg, oscillations in vacuum, in matter of constant density, with non-standard neutrino interactions, and in diverse new-physics scenarios.  The method does not apply to scenarios where neutrinos ``leak out'' of the system, \eg,  with neutrino decays into invisible products\ \cite{Joshipura:2002fb, Beacom:2002cb, Beacom:2002vi, GonzalezGarcia:2008ru, Baerwald:2012kc, Berryman:2014yoa}, or open systems, like those with decoherence\ \cite{Benatti:2000ph, Lisi:2000zt, Ohlsson:2000mj, Carpio:2017nui, Coloma:2018idr}.  Whether a system leaks, however, depends on how much of it one chooses to describe.  A 3+1 sterile-neutrino scenario\ \cite{Abazajian:2012ys, Palazzo:2013me, Gariazzo:2015rra, Dentler:2018sju, Giunti:2019aiy} appears to leak when it is written in three flavors, because the fourth state then sits outside the description and probability is not conserved within it; over all four states it is closed and unitary, and meets both conditions above exactly.  Decay to invisible products and genuinely open systems are different: no enlargement of the flavor basis makes them closed, and they lie outside the method for good.

We provide the computer code {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact}, a lightweight numerical implementation of the OS method that computes exact two-, three-, and four-flavor oscillation probabilities for arbitrary time-independent Hamiltonians.  The code can be easily used in oscillation analyses.

In Section \ref{section:scope}, we set the scope, context, and approach of the paper.  In Section \ref{section:general_remarks}, we recap the basics of neutrino oscillations and establish the concrete goal of the computation.  In Sections \ref{section:two_flavors} and \ref{section:three_flavors}, we present the OS method, in a simplified formulation, for systems of two and three flavors.  In Section \ref{section:code}, we describe {\tt NuOscProbExact} and show examples of its use.  In Section \ref{section:conclusions}, we conclude.

\section{Scope, context, and approach}
\label{section:scope}

Below, to compute the oscillation probabilities, we follow the OS method.  References\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb, Ohlsson:1999um, Ohlsson:2000zz, Ohlsson:2001et} introduced expressions applicable to generic oscillation scenarios, and also found analytic expressions\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb} for the probabilities in the cases of two-flavor oscillations in matter and three-flavor oscillations in vacuum and matter.  Reference \cite{Barger:1980tf} presented an earlier application to three-flavor oscillations in matter.  Because the exact analytic expressions tailored to three-neutrino oscillations in vacuum and matter are lengthy, and because we are interested in providing a general-purpose numerical implementation of the method, we do not attempt to reproduce analytical solutions or find new ones.  

Later, we work through the method.  Here, we give an overview.  We start by expanding the Hamiltonian in terms of $2 \times 2$ Pauli matrices --- in the case of two neutrino flavors --- or of $3 \times 3$ Gell-Mann matrices --- in the case of three flavors, or of the fifteen $4 \times 4$ generators of SU(4), in the case of four.  \ref{appendix:matrices} shows the first two sets of these matrices.  When studying neutrino oscillations, these expansions are sometimes performed not on the Hamiltonian, but on the associated density matrix.  This approach is particularly useful to study oscillations in the early Universe\ \cite{McKellar:1992ja, Bell:1998ds, Hannestad:2001iy, Wong:2002fa, Hannestad:2012ky, Boriero:2017tkh} and in supernovae\ \cite{Pantaleone:1994ns, Sigl:1994hc, Pastor:2002we, Balantekin:2004ug, Fogli:2007bk, Dasgupta:2007ws, Duan:2010bg, Mirizzi:2015eza}.  

In the OS method, we instead first expand the Hamiltonian $H$ and then the associated time-evolution operator $e^{-i H t}$.  For the latter, we use the exponential expansions of Pauli and Gell-Mann matrices\ \cite{MacFarlane:1968vc, Curtright:2015iba, VanKortryk:2015kua}.  These expansions are a direct application of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, which states that an analytic function of an $n \times n$ matrix can be written as a polynomial of degree $(n-1)$ in that matrix.  The coefficients of the expansion are computed using SU(2), SU(3), and SU(4) invariants, which allows us to bypass the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian that would otherwise be needed to compute the probabilities.

Sophisticated numerical codes exist to compute probabilities, either for general application or for particular scenarios, \eg, {\tt GLoBES}\ \cite{GLoBES}, {\tt nuCraft}\ \cite{nuCraft}, {\tt NuSQuIDS}\ \cite{NuSQuIDS}, and {\tt Prob3++}\ \cite{Prob3pp}.  The general-purpose code {\tt NuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS} expands the same SU($n$) generator basis, but applies it to the density matrix rather than to the state, and integrates the resulting equations of motion numerically.  That is a strictly larger problem: it admits time dependence, interactions, and non-unitary terms, at the cost of solving a differential equation.  The relationship is that the construction used here is the analytic kernel of those equations, restricted to closed systems at constant density; \ref{section:general_n} makes the statement precise.  The code {\tt Prob3++}\ \cite{Prob3pp} implements the expansions for oscillations in matter, based on \Ref\ \cite{Barger:1980tf}.

The OS papers treat two and three flavors, and observe that beyond three the characteristic equation must be solved numerically, ``since only characteristic equations up to degree four are analytically solvable''\ \cite{Kusnezov:1995, VanKortryk:2015kua}.  That remark identifies $n=4$ as reachable without carrying it out; Section \ref{section:four_flavors} carries it out, and {\tt NuOscProbExact} implements it.  We do not present the four-flavor case as an OS result, because it is not one: it follows from a statement that holds at any $n$, of which the OS expansion is one projection, and which we set out first in Section \ref{section:general_n}.  \ \Ref\ \cite{Kamo:2002sj} had earlier reached the $n=4$ case for four-flavor oscillations in matter, and {\tt NuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS} treats up to $n=6$\ \cite{Salvado:Private}.

Below, our approach is expository while condensed: we provide sufficient detail to present the method and facilitate its implementation, and refer to earlier works for further mathematical detail.

\section{Neutrino oscillation recap}
\label{section:general_remarks}

Let $\nu$ represent the flavor state of a neutrino.  The state evolves according to the Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:evolution_eq}
 i \frac{d\nu}{dt} = H \nu \;,
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the time elapsed since the creation of the neutrino and $H$ is the Hamiltonian written in flavor space.  We use units where $c = \hbar = 1$.  By definition, $H$ is Hermitian.  In a system of $n$ neutrinos, we represent $H$ by a $n \times n$ matrix and $\nu$ by a column vector with $n$ entries.  Below, we consider the cases $n=2$, for two-neutrino oscillations, and $n=3$, for three-neutrino oscillations.  The four-flavor case, $n=4$, is discussed in Section \ref{section:scope}.

We restrict the discussion to time-independent Hamiltonians, so that the corresponding time-evolution operator is $U\left(t\right) = e^{-iHt}$.  Hamiltonians of this type describe, for instance, neutrino propagation in vacuum and in matter of constant density.  Because neutrinos are relativistic, we approximate the propagated distance $L \simeq t$.   Thus, the evolved state of a neutrino born as $\nu_\alpha$ ($\alpha = e, \mu, \tau$) is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:evolved_state}
 \nu_\alpha\left(L\right) 
 = U(L) \nu_\alpha
 = e^{-iHL} \nu_\alpha \;.
\end{equation}
Since $H$ is Hermitian, the evolution operator $U$ is unitary.

Because the Hamiltonian in flavor space is non-diagonal, \ie, because it mixes flavor states, after propagating for a distance $L$, the neutrino of initial flavor $\nu_\alpha$ becomes a superposition of neutrinos of all flavors, each with a different probability amplitude, $\nu_\beta^\dagger \nu_\alpha(L)$ ($\beta = e, \mu, \tau$).  The probability of detecting the neutrino with flavor $\beta$ is $P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}(L) = \lvert \nu_\beta^\dagger \nu_\alpha(L) \rvert^2$.

In \equ{evolved_state}, to compute the action of the evolution operator, $\nu_\alpha$ must be an eigenstate of $H$.  Yet, this is typically not the case.  Thus, the usual procedure to compute the evolved state is to diagonalize the Hamiltonian in \equ{evolved_state}, compute the evolved state in the space spanned by the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian, and rotate back to flavor space to obtain $\nu_\alpha(L)$.  These steps are often carried out numerically, especially in the three-neutrino case, because the expressions quickly become unmanageable.  There are numerical codes that do this efficiently, \eg, {\tt GLoBES}\ \cite{GLoBES, Kopp:2006wp, Huber:2007ji}.

Below, we follow instead the OS method, as explained in Section\ \ref{section:scope}, implement it numerically, and show results of the implementation.

\section{One construction, and its projections}
\label{section:general_n}

\label{section:general_n}

Let $\mathbb{H}$ be an arbitrary Hermitian $n \times n$ Hamiltonian in the flavor basis, constant over a baseline $L$, and let $\tilde{\mathbb{H}}$ be its traceless part, whose eigenvalues we write as $\psi_m$ and whose characteristic polynomial is $\chi(\psi) = \det(\psi \mathbb{1} - \tilde{\mathbb{H}})$.  For a non-degenerate spectrum, the evolution operator is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:sylvester}
 \mathbb{U}(L)
 =
 \sum_{m=1}^n e^{-i\psi_m L} \mathbb{P}_m \;,
 \qquad
 \mathbb{P}_m
 =
 \frac{{\rm adj}\left(\psi_m \mathbb{1} - \tilde{\mathbb{H}}\right)}{\chi^\prime\left(\psi_m\right)} \;,
\end{equation}
with $\mathbb{P}_m$ the orthogonal projector onto the $m$-th eigenspace.  This is Sylvester's interpolation formula\ \cite{Sylvester:1883} --- already invoked in Section \ref{section:three_flavors} for the $3 \times 3$ case --- and $\mathbb{P}_m$ are the Frobenius covariants; the underlying statements are nineteenth-century.

Equation (\ref{equ:sylvester}) is one object, and several methods in the oscillation literature are readings of it.  Contracting it with the trace metric, $u_0 = \frac{1}{n}{\rm Tr}\,\mathbb{U}$ and $u_a = \frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(\lambda^a \mathbb{U})$, keeps the whole matrix in the generator basis and gives the OS expansion.  Keeping instead its columns gives the adjugate method\ \cite{Sylvester:1883}; keeping its diagonal gives the eigenvector-eigenvalue identity; writing it as a polynomial with Vandermonde-determined coefficients gives the Cayley-Hamilton form used in \Ref\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb}.  What differs between them is which slice is kept, and in which basis --- not the mathematics.  Seen this way, the OS coefficients are the SU($n$)-basis components of the adjugate, and the extension to another value of $n$ requires no new idea.

Carrying this out, $u_0 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_m e^{-i\psi_m L}$ and
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:ua_general}
 u_a
 =
 \sum_{m=1}^n e^{-i\psi_m L}
 \frac{N_a\left(\psi_m\right)}{\chi^\prime\left(\psi_m\right)} \;,
 \quad
 N_a(\psi) \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\!\left[\lambda^a\,{\rm adj}\left(\psi\mathbb{1}-\tilde{\mathbb{H}}\right)\right] \;.
\end{equation}
The numerator $N_a$ has degree $n-2$ in $\psi$, and its coefficients are $h$, $h \ast h$, $\left(h \ast h\right) \ast h$, and so on: a star-product tower of length $n-1$, terminated by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem.  Everything follows from traces of powers of $\tilde{\mathbb{H}}$; the $d$ tensor is never built at runtime, and no eigenvectors are ever required.  The eigenvalues are the only non-polynomial input.

Two conventions follow from \equ{sylvester} rather than from any implementation, and both are worth stating here because they are where users of any such code go wrong.

The first is the antineutrino rule.  Antineutrinos need no separate construction: conjugating the vacuum term and reversing the sign of every matter potential,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:antineutrino}
 \mathbb{H}\left(\bar{\nu}\right)
 =
 \left[\mathbb{H}^{\rm vac}\right]^\ast - \mathbb{A} - \mathbb{V} \;,
\end{equation}
gives a Hamiltonian that \equ{sylvester} then treats like any other.  The conjugation reverses the sign of $\delta_{\rm CP}$, and with it every $T$-odd term; the reversal of $\mathbb{A}$ and $\mathbb{V}$ is what makes the matter resonance appear for one sign of $\Delta m_{31}^2$ in neutrinos and the other in antineutrinos.  Both halves are needed, and applying only one of them is the commonest error in this kind of calculation.  In the coefficient language the rule is simpler still: $h_a$ is unchanged for the symmetric generators and reversed for the antisymmetric ones, so the antineutrino probabilities are read off the tables below by exchanging each entry with its transpose.

The second is that the eigenvalues are the only quantity in \equ{sylvester} that is not a polynomial in the Hamiltonian.  Everything else --- the interpolation, the projectors, the probabilities --- is rational in them.  That is why the construction extends to any $n$ for which the eigenvalues can be had, why it stops where they cannot be had in closed form, and why the accuracy of the whole thing is set by the accuracy of the roots.  Sections \ref{section:four_flavors} and \ref{section:performance} return to each of those in turn.

\section{Two-neutrino oscillations}
\label{section:two_flavors}

We consider first oscillations between only two neutrino flavors; later, we consider three flavors.  This is a good approximation when describing reactor, accelerator, and atmospheric neutrinos.  We represent the two-neutrino Hamiltonian operator by a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\mathbb{H}_2$.  The three traceless, Hermitian Pauli matrices $\sigma^k$ ($k=1,2,3$) --- the generators of the SU(2) algebra --- plus the identity matrix $\mathbb{1}$ make up the orthogonal basis of $2 \times 2$ matrices.  Thus, we expand the Hamiltonian as
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h2_expansion}
 \mathbb{H}_2 = h_0 \mathbb{1} + h_k \sigma^k \;,
\end{equation}
where, here and below, we assume the Einstein convention of summing over repeated indices.  The coefficients $h_0$ and $h_k$ are functions of the components of the Hamiltonian; we show their explicit expressions in Table \ref{tab:h2_coefficients}.  In the two-neutrino case, the neutrino state at any time is $\nu(L) = f_\alpha(L) \nu_\alpha + f_\beta(L) \nu_\beta$, where $f_\alpha$ and $f_\beta$ are, respectively, the probability amplitudes of measuring the state to be a $\nu_\alpha$ or a $\nu_\beta$ (with $\alpha \neq \beta$).  We represent the neutrino state as a two-component column vector; the pure states are $\nu_\alpha = \left(1~0\right)^{\rm T}$ and $\nu_\beta = \left(0~1\right)^{\rm T}$.

Thus, the evolution operator is $\mathbb{U}_2\left(L\right) = e^{-i \mathbb{H}_2 L} = e^{-i(h_0 \mathbb{1}+h_k\sigma^k)L}$.  We factorize\footnote{We can do this because the commutator $C_2\equiv\left[h_0 \mathbb{1},h_k\sigma^k\right]=0$, so that $\left[h_0 \mathbb{1}, C_2\right]=\left[h_k\sigma^k, C_2\right]=0$.  For analogous reasons, we can also do this in the three-neutrino case in Section \ref{section:three_flavors}.} this into $e^{-ih_0\mathbb{1}L} e^{-ih_k\sigma^kL}$.  The operator $e^{-ih_0\mathbb{1}L}$ introduces a global phase that does not affect the probability, \ie, $e^{-ih_0\mathbb{1}L}\nu =e^{-ih_0L}\nu$.  After discarding it, we are left with $\mathbb{U}_2(L) =  e^{-ih_k\sigma^kL}$. 

To compute the action of $\mathbb{U}_2$, we use a well-known identity of Pauli matrices that generalizes Euler's formula,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:pauli_identity}
 e^{\pm i a_k \sigma^k}
 =
 \cos\left(\left\vert a \right\vert \right) 
 \pm 
 i \hat{a}_k\sigma^k\sin\left(\left\vert a\right\vert \right) \;, 
\end{equation}
where $\hat{a}$ is a unit vector in the direction of the vector $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and $\lvert a \rvert$ is its modulus.  So we can write the evolution operator as in \Ref\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb},
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:evol_op_2nu}
 \mathbb{U}_2\left(L\right) 
 =
 \cos\left(\left\vert h\right\vert L\right) \mathbb{1}
 -
 i \frac{\sin\left(\left\vert h\right\vert L\right)}{\lvert h \rvert} h_k\sigma^k  \;,
\end{equation}
where $\lvert h \rvert^2 \equiv \lvert h_1 \rvert^2 + \lvert h_2 \rvert^2 + \lvert h_3 \rvert^2$.

The evolved state $\nu_\alpha (L)$ of a neutrino that was created with flavor $\alpha$, \ie, with $f_\alpha(0) = 1$ and $f_\beta(0) = 0$, is $\nu_\alpha\left(L\right) = \mathbb{U}_2(L) \nu_\alpha$.  After some manipulation, the flavor-transition probability $P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}(L) = \lvert \nu_\beta^\dagger \mathbb{U}_2(L) \nu_\alpha \rvert^2$ is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:prob_2nu_general}
 P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}(L)
 = \frac{\left\vert h_1 \right\vert^2 + \left\vert h_2 \right\vert^2}
 {\left\vert h\right\vert^2} \sin^2\left(\left\vert h\right\vert L\right) \;\; (\alpha \neq \beta) \;,
\end{equation}
where $\lvert h_1 \rvert^2 + \lvert h_2 \rvert^2 = \lvert (\mathbb{H}_2)_{12} \rvert^2$ and $\lvert h \rvert^2 = \lvert (\mathbb{H}_2)_{12} \rvert^2 + \lvert (\mathbb{H}_2)_{11} - (\mathbb{H}_2)_{22} \rvert^2 / 4$.  Because of the conservation of probability, $P_{\nu_\alpha \rightarrow \nu_\alpha}\left(L\right) = 1 - P_{\nu_\alpha \rightarrow \nu_\beta}\left(L\right)$.  \ref{appendix:derivation_prob_2nu_general} contains the derivation of \equ{prob_2nu_general}.  \ref{section:2nu_vacuum} shows a simple application to two-flavor oscillations in vacuum.  Equation (\ref{equ:prob_2nu_general}) is our final result in the two-neutrino case.  

The key to the calculation of \equ{prob_2nu_general} was to expand the time-evolution operator via the Pauli-matrix identity, \equ{pauli_identity}.  Later, in the three-neutrino case, we use an analogous identity for the Gell-Mann matrices.

We follow the steps that we used in the two-neutrino case closely.  We represent the three-neutrino Hamiltonian by a $3 \times 3$ matrix $\mathbb{H}_3$.  The eight traceless, Hermitian Gell-Mann matrices $\lambda^k$ ($k=1,\ldots,8$) --- with $\lambda^k/2$ the generators of the SU(3) algebra --- plus the identity matrix $\mathbb{1}$ make up the orthogonal basis of $3 \times 3$ matrices.  Thus, we expand the Hamiltonian as
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_expansion}
 \mathbb{H}_3 = h_0 \mathbb{1} + h_k \lambda^k \;,
\end{equation}
where $h_0$ and $h_k$ are now functions of the components of $\mathbb{H}_3$; we show their explicit expressions in Table \ref{tab:h3_coefficients}.  The neutrino state at any time is $\nu(L) = f_e(L) \nu_e + f_\mu(L) \nu_\mu + f_\tau(L) \nu_\tau$, where $f_e$, $f_\mu$, and $f_\tau$ are, respectively, the probability amplitudes of measuring the state to be a $\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$, or $\nu_\tau$.  We represent the neutrino state as a three-component column vector; the pure states are $\nu_e = \left(1~0~0\right)^{\rm T}$, $\nu_\mu=\left(0~1~0\right)^{\rm T}$, and $\nu_\tau=\left(0~0~1\right)^{\rm T}$. 

The evolution operator is $\mathbb{U}_3\left(L\right) = e^{-i \mathbb{H}_3 L} = e^{-ih_0\mathbb{1}L}e^{-ih_k\lambda^kL}$.   Again, after discarding the global phase, we are left with $\mathbb{U}_3\left(L\right) = e^{-ih_k\lambda^kL}$.  

Next we compute the action of $\mathbb{U}_3$ on a neutrino state.  We wish to expand $\mathbb{U}_3$ using an identity for the Gell-Mann matrices that is similar to the identity for the Pauli matrices, \equ{pauli_identity}, and that allows us to write
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:u3_def}
 \mathbb{U}_3\left(L\right) 
 = 
 u_0 \mathbb{1} + i u_k \lambda^k \;,
\end{equation}
where the complex coefficients $u_0$ and $u_k$ are functions of $L$ and the $h_k$.  Reference\ \cite{MacFarlane:1968vc} introduced and demonstrated such an identity; below, we make use of their results, leaving most of the proofs to the reference.  See also \Refs\ \cite{Lehrer-Ilamed:1964, Rosen:1971nt, Torruella:1975, Kusnezov:1995, Curtright:2015iba, VanKortryk:2015kua} for further details.

The coefficients in \equ{u3_def} can be trivially written as $u_0=\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}~\mathbb{U}_3$ and $u_k=-\frac{i}{2}\text{Tr}(\lambda^k \mathbb{U}_3)$; next we unpack these forms.  An application of Sylvester's formula\ \cite{Sylvester:1883} to $3 \times 3$ matrices allows us to express the coefficients in terms of the SU(3) invariants
\begin{eqnarray*}
 \label{equ:def_I2}
 && L^2 \left\vert h \right\vert^2  \equiv L^2 h_kh^k \;, \\
 \label{equ:def_I3}
 && -L^3 \langle h \rangle \equiv -L^3 d_{ijk}h^ih^jh^k \;.
\end{eqnarray*}
The tensor $d_{ijk} = \frac{1}{4} \text{Tr} \left( \lbrace\lambda_i,\lambda_j\rbrace\lambda_k \right)$, where the brackets represent the anticommutator.  It appears in the product law of Gell-Mann matrices and its components are the structure constants of the SU(3) algebra.  Table \ref{tab:d_tensor} shows all non-zero components.

Next, we solve the characteristic equation of $-h_k\lambda^kL$, \ie, $\phi^3-(L^2 \left\vert h \right\vert^2) \phi-\frac{2}{3} (-L^3 \langle h \rangle)=0$.  The equation follows from the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, written conveniently in terms of invariants\ \cite{MacFarlane:1968vc, Curtright:2015iba}.  Its three latent roots, or eigenvalues, are $\phi_m \equiv \psi_m L$ ($m=1,2,3$), with
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:phi_alpha}
 \psi_m
 \equiv
 \frac{2 \left\vert h \right\vert}{\sqrt{3}} \cos\left[\frac{1}{3}\left(\chi+2\pi m \right)\right] \;,
\end{equation}
where $\cos\left(\chi\right) = -\sqrt{3} \langle h \rangle / \lvert h \rvert^3$.  The step above is key: writing the eigenvalues in terms of the SU(3) invariants allows us to bypass an explicit diagonalization\ \cite{Curtright:2015iba}.

With this, the coefficients in \equ{u3_def} are
\begin{eqnarray}
 && \label{equ:u0}
 u_0=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{m=1}^3 e^{i L \psi_m} \;, \\
 \label{equ:uk_expanded}
 && u_k
 =
 \sum_{m=1}^3
 e^{i L \psi_m} \frac{\psi_m h_k - \left(h \ast h\right)_k}{3\psi_m^2 - \left\vert h \right\vert^2} \;,
\end{eqnarray}
where $\left(h\ast h\right)_i \equiv d_{ijk}h^jh^k$.  \ref{appendix:derivation_uk_expanded} contains the derivation of \equ{uk_expanded}.  Using Eqs.\ (\ref{equ:phi_alpha}), (\ref{equ:u0}), and (\ref{equ:uk_expanded}), we write the evolution operator concisely as in \Ref\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb},
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:u3_expanded}
 \mathbb{U}_3(L)
 =
 \sum_{m=1}^3
 e^{i L \psi_m}
 \left[
 \mathbb{1} 
 +
 \frac{\psi_m h_k - \left( h \ast h \right)_k}{3\psi_m^2-\lvert h \rvert^2} \lambda^k
 \right] \;.
\end{equation}
Equation \ref{equ:u3_expanded} was introduced in \Refs \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb, Ohlsson:1999um, Ohlsson:2000zz}, and applied to find analytic expressions of the probabilities in the cases of oscillations in vacuum and matter.  For a numerical implementation of \equ{u3_expanded} it is convenient to calculate the coefficients $u_0$ and $u_k$ with Eqs.\ (\ref{equ:u0}) and (\ref{equ:uk_expanded}), and use them to directly expand $\mathbb{U}_3$ in \equ{u3_def}.  This is the strategy that we adopt in {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact}.

As pointed out by \Ref\ \cite{MacFarlane:1968vc}, this may seem a disappointing generalization of \equ{pauli_identity} to SU(3).  However, when constructing an exponential parametrization of SU(3), there is no way to avoid the solution of at least a cubic equation. \section{Four-neutrino oscillations}
\label{section:four_flavors}

The two- and three-flavor constructions of Sections \ref{section:two_flavors} and \ref{section:three_flavors} are due to OS.  The four-flavor one is not: it is the case $n=4$ of Section \ref{section:general_n}, and it is the last one for which the eigenvalues can be written down in closed form.

\subsection{The four-flavor case}
\label{section:4nu_case}

SU(4) has rank three, so $\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_4$ carries three invariants, all obtainable from traces,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:invariants_4nu}
 I_2 = \lvert h \rvert^2 \;, \quad
 I_3 = \langle h \rangle \;, \quad
 I_4 = \tfrac{1}{2}\left({\rm Tr}\,\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_4^4 - I_2^2\right) \;,
\end{equation}
the first two being those of the three-flavor case.  The characteristic equation is the depressed quartic $\chi(\psi) = \psi^4 - I_2\psi^2 - \frac{2}{3}I_3\psi + \frac{1}{4}(I_2^2 - 2I_4) = 0$, which Ferrari-Euler reduction turns into the resolvent cubic
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:resolvent_cubic}
 z^3 - 2I_2 z^2 + 2I_4 z - \tfrac{4}{9}I_3^2 = 0 \;.
\end{equation}
Its roots are $z_i = (\psi_j+\psi_k)^2$, real and non-negative precisely because $\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_4$ is Hermitian.  Equation (\ref{equ:resolvent_cubic}) therefore yields to \emph{exactly} the trigonometric formula already used at three flavors, \equ{phi_alpha}, and
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:psi_4nu}
 \psi_m = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(s_1\sqrt{z_1}+s_2\sqrt{z_2}+s_3\sqrt{z_3}\right) \;,
 \quad
 s_i = \pm 1 \;,
\end{equation}
with the signs fixed by $s_1s_2s_3\sqrt{z_1z_2z_3} = \frac{2}{3}I_3$.  The three-flavor machinery sits inside the four-flavor solution; this is the sense in which the extension is inexpensive.

Two accidents that make $n=3$ compact both fail here.  The rank is three rather than two, so $I_4$ appears alongside $I_2$ and $I_3$.  And the SU(3) identity $\left(h \ast h\right) \ast h = \frac{1}{3}\lvert h \rvert^2 h$, which truncates the tower of \equ{ua_general} at one rung, is a Cayley-Hamilton accident of $n=3$: at $n=4$ the theorem constrains $\tilde{\mathbb{H}}_4^4$, and hence $(h \ast h) \ast (h \ast h)$, but leaves $(h \ast h) \ast h$ free.  Numerically the identity is violated at the level of tens of per cent for random $h$, so the second rung enters as independent data.  With that, Eqs.\ (\ref{equ:u0}) and (\ref{equ:uk_expanded}) become
\begin{eqnarray}
 \label{equ:u0_4nu}
 && u_0 = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{m=1}^4 e^{iL\psi_m} \;, \\
 \label{equ:uk_4nu}
 && u_k
 =
 i\sum_{m=1}^4
 e^{iL\psi_m}
 \frac{\left(\psi_m^2 - \frac{1}{2}I_2\right)h_k - \psi_m \left(h \ast h\right)_k + \left(\left(h \ast h\right)\ast h\right)_k}
      {4\psi_m^3 - 2I_2\psi_m + \frac{2}{3}I_3} \;,
\end{eqnarray}
in the same convention as Section \ref{section:three_flavors}: the $\psi_m$ are the latent roots of $-h_k\lambda^kL$ divided by $L$, and $\mathbb{U}_4 = u_0\mathbb{1} + iu_k\lambda^k$ as in \equ{u3_def}.  The denominator is $\chi^\prime(\psi_m)$, exactly as the $3\psi_m^2 - \lvert h \rvert^2$ of \equ{uk_expanded} is.  Comparing the two makes the pattern of \equ{ua_general} concrete: one more power of $\psi_m$ in the numerator, and one more rung of the star tower.

The closed-form SU(4) exponential is not new outside neutrino physics: \Refs\ \cite{VanKortryk:2015kua} give the SU(4) and SU(5) exponentials directly in the trace invariants of \equ{invariants_4nu}.  Within the four-flavor oscillation problem, the characteristic quartic has been arrived at more than once\ \cite{Kamo:2002sj}.  What we add is the implementation, and the probability table below.

\subsection{Probabilities, and what four flavors buys}

Fixing the generator basis, the probabilities take the same shape at every $n$.  For each unordered flavor pair exactly two generators are supported, one symmetric and one antisymmetric; the remaining $n-1$ are the Cartan generators.  Table \ref{tab:prob_4nu} gives the result at $n=4$.  Three structural features follow for any $n$, and are useful as run-time checks: the off-diagonal entries come in $T$-conjugate pairs differing only in the sign of the antisymmetric coefficient, so that $T$ violation resides entirely there; unitarity is inherited rather than imposed, appearing as the constraints $\sum_\beta P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} = 1$; and the number of physical Dirac phases entering the off-diagonal entries is $(n-1)(n-2)/2$ --- none at $n=2$, one at $n=3$, and three at $n=4$.

Because \equ{uk_4nu} takes an arbitrary Hermitian $\mathbb{H}_4$, it covers short-baseline 3+1 fits, the sterile matter resonance for Earth-crossing neutrinos, and 3+1 combined with NSI or LIV, with no further derivation.  In constant matter, after removing the flavor-universal neutral-current potential as a global phase, the potential matrix is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:a4}
 \mathbb{A}_4 = {\rm diag}\left(V_{\rm CC}, 0, 0, V_s\right) \;,
 \quad
 V_s = -V_{\rm NC} = \frac{G_{\rm F} n_n}{\sqrt{2}} \;,
\end{equation}
so that $V_s \simeq V_{\rm CC}/2$ in the crust.  The sterile state does not feel the charged-current potential, but the other three do not feel $V_s$; it is that difference that drives the resonance shown later in \figu{sterile_earth}.

\subsection{Why the construction stops at four}
\label{section:beyond_four}

At $n \geq 5$ the Abel-Ruffini theorem removes the closed-form-in-radicals eigenvalues: the general quintic is not soluble in radicals, because the symmetric group $S_5$ is not soluble while $S_2$, $S_3$, and $S_4$ are.  The obstruction is a theorem, not a gap in anyone's algebra, and it is external to neutrino physics entirely.

Three remarks calibrate what is actually lost.  First, closed form survives, uselessly: the general quintic is solvable beyond radicals, through the Bring radical and elliptic modular functions or through hypergeometric functions, but those special functions are themselves evaluated iteratively, so nothing is gained over applying Newton's method to $\chi$.  The radicals were never the point; the eigenvalue-only structure was.  Second, and consequently, the practical scheme at $n \geq 5$ is to seed the eigenvalues --- from an approximation, or from the closed-form $n \leq 4$ solution of a decoupled sub-block when a hierarchy permits --- and polish them with Newton's method; the assembly in \equ{sylvester} then proceeds unchanged.  Third, everything structural continues: \equ{ua_general} needs only that $\psi_m$ be a root of $\chi$, not that it be available in radicals.  What ends at four is the formula, not the method.

\section{Code description and examples}
\label{section:code}

{\it Description.---}  The code {\tt NuOscProbExact} that we provide is a lightweight numerical implementation of the OS method described above.  It computes exact oscillation probabilities in the often-studied two- and three-flavor cases, and in the four-flavor case, for arbitrary time-independent Hamiltonians.  (For time-dependent Hamiltonians, see {\tt NuSQuIDS}\ \cite{NuSQuIDS}.  A density profile that varies smoothly, rather than in steps, is better served by a Magnus-type method than by the slab decomposition described below.)   {\tt NuOscProbExact} is fully written in {\tt Python}, and requires version 3.9 or later; it is open source, and publicly available in a GitHub repository\ \cite{NuOscProbExact}.

The main input to {\tt NuOscProbExact} is the Hamiltonian matrix $\mathbb{H}_2$, $\mathbb{H}_3$, or $\mathbb{H}_4$, provided as a $2 \times 2$, $3 \times 3$, or $4 \times 4$ array or nested list.  The code internally computes the $h_k$ coefficients using Table \ref{tab:h2_coefficients} in the two-neutrino case and Table \ref{tab:h3_coefficients} in the three-neutrino case.  To compute two-neutrino probabilities, the code evaluates \equ{prob_2nu_general}.  To compute three-neutrino probabilities, the code evaluates the expressions in Table \ref{tab:prob_3nu}.

The same routines also accept a stack of Hamiltonians, an array of baselines, or both, broadcast against each other, and evaluate them without a Python-level loop.  For the scans of energy that produce \figu{prob_3nu} and \figu{prob_2nu}, and for oscillograms in energy and arrival direction, this is roughly one to two orders of magnitude faster than calling the scalar routine in a loop.  An optional compiled backend, enabled by installing the {\tt numba} extra, gives a further factor of about $4$--$5$ on top of that, and agrees with the pure-{\tt numpy} path to round-off; it is used only above the stack sizes where it has been measured to win, which is every stack at three and four flavors and only large ones at two.  Section \ref{section:performance} quantifies all of this.  By default the code also checks that the Hamiltonian it is given is Hermitian, and raises an error if it is not, rather than silently returning quantities that still sum to one.

\smallskip

{\it Documentation.---}  The main source of documentation is the accompanying site\ \cite{NuOscProbExactDocs}; a copy is bundled with the code.  Besides an API reference generated from the source, it carries three things that do not fit in a paper: a methodology section that works through the construction of Sections \ref{section:general_n}--\ref{section:four_flavors} in the form in which it is implemented, including the conditioning behavior discussed below; a set of numerical recipes for the cases users actually meet --- layered profiles, oscillograms, antineutrinos, parameter scans; and eighteen executable notebooks, one of which generates every figure in this paper.  The notebooks are run as part of the test suite, so the documented behavior and the shipped behavior cannot drift apart.

The optional compiled backend is installed with {\tt pip install nuoscprobexact[fast]}, which adds {\tt numba}.

{\it Validation.---}  The code ships with a test suite of 596 tests, which covers every line and branch of the library.  Beyond internal consistency, the results are checked against independent sources: against {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS}, an independently written code, with which the three-flavor probabilities agree to $10^{-11}$ and the four-flavor ones to $10^{-9}$ once conventions are matched; against the closed-form eigenvalues of Zaglauer \& Schwarzer\ \cite{Zaglauer:1988gz} for the three-flavor matter spectrum; against direct numerical exponentiation of the Hamiltonian, as in \figu{validation}; and, in the two-flavor vacuum case, against the standard analytic formula.  Where a residual remains --- as in the four-flavor case, whose eigenvalue cluster can be stiff --- the tests establish which side of the comparison it belongs to rather than absorbing it into a loose tolerance.

\smallskip

{\it Limitations.---}  Two kinds are worth separating: those inherent to the method, and those of this implementation.

The method assumes what Section \ref{Section_Introduction} states: a closed system, and a Hamiltonian that is constant along the path.  Neither is a limitation that better programming would remove.  Decay to invisible products and genuinely open systems --- decoherence, and any Lindblad term --- fall outside it, and require the density-matrix formalism that {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS} integrates.  A non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, used to describe absorption, is a subtler case: \equ{sylvester} survives formally, but everything downstream that used the reality of the spectrum does not, including the trigonometric root formulas and the identification of the moduli with probabilities.  A density that varies continuously is handled here only by approximating it as piece-wise constant; for a smoothly varying profile a Magnus-type integrator is the better tool, with \equ{sylvester} supplying its exponential map at each step.  And, as Section \ref{section:beyond_four} sets out, the closed form ends at four flavors.

Of this implementation, four things should be known.  First and most important, the closed form is not the most accurate route near a level crossing, which is exactly where the interesting physics is.  Forming the characteristic polynomial from traces and then extracting its roots costs a power of the relative spectral gap: if $r$ eigenvalues cluster, the error in the roots grows as $g^{-(r-1)}$, where $g$ is the gap in units of the spectral spread.  This is the classical instability of extracting eigenvalues from a characteristic polynomial, and it is not repairable within the closed form --- neither by a more careful polynomial evaluation nor by the standard divided-difference remedy, because the error is already in the roots.  Where a spectrum is nearly degenerate, a backward-stable eigensolver is the better choice, and its error does not depend on the gap at all.  Second, exact degeneracy is handled by clustering roots that lie within a tolerance of one another and taking the interpolation over distinct clusters; without that step the formulas divide by zero.  Third, the electron density is computed as stated in Section \ref{section:prob_3nu_matter}, which is about $0.8\%$ low.  Fourth, the mixing parameters carried by the code are pinned to NuFit 4.0, deliberately, so that the examples here remain reproducible.

It is also worth being plain about what the method is and is not good for, since ``no diagonalization'' is easy to oversell.  It is branch-free, vectorizes well, accepts an arbitrary Hermitian Hamiltonian, and differentiates exactly --- the last being the advantage that survives scrutiny, since the derivatives of the probabilities follow from implicit differentiation of the characteristic polynomial with no eigenvector ever differentiated.  It is not, at three flavors, the fastest route for a production long-baseline fit, and it is not the most accurate one near a resonance.  Nor does ``no eigenvectors are required'' mean they are never wanted: the mixing matrix in matter, and with it the matter-dressed angles and resonance conditions, is eigenvector information, and obtaining it means taking one of the other readings of \equ{sylvester}.

\subsection{Oscillations in vacuum}

The Hamiltonian that drives oscillations in vacuum is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_vacuum}
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm vac}(E)
 = 
 \frac{1}{2E} \left( \mathbb{R}_{3,\theta} \mathbb{M}_3^2 \mathbb{R}_{3,\theta}^\dagger \right) \;,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{M}_3^2 \equiv {\rm diag}(0, \Delta m_{21}^2, \Delta m_{31}^2)$ is the mass matrix, with $\Delta m_{21}^2 \equiv m_2^2-m_1^2$ and $\Delta m_{31}^2 \equiv m_3^2-m_1^2$, and the $3 \times 3$ complex rotation matrix $\mathbb{R}_{3,\theta}$ is the Pontecorvo-Maki-Nakagawa-Sakata (PMNS) mixing matrix.  We express it in terms of three mixing angles, $\theta_{13}$, $\theta_{12}$, $\theta_{23}$, and one CP-violation phase, $\delta_{\rm CP}$\ \cite{Tanabashi:2018oca}.  Conventions differ between codes, and are the commonest source of disagreement between them, so we write ours out.  With $c_{ij} \equiv \cos\theta_{ij}$ and $s_{ij} \equiv \sin\theta_{ij}$, and $\delta \equiv \delta_{\rm CP}$,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:pmns}
 \mathbb{R}_{3,\theta}
 =
 \left(
 \begin{array}{ccc}
  c_{12}c_{13} & s_{12}c_{13} & s_{13}e^{-i\delta} \\
  -s_{12}c_{23}-c_{12}s_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta} & c_{12}c_{23}-s_{12}s_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta} & s_{23}c_{13} \\
  s_{12}s_{23}-c_{12}c_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta} & -c_{12}s_{23}-s_{12}c_{23}s_{13}e^{i\delta} & c_{23}c_{13}
 \end{array}
 \right) ,
\end{equation}
which is the standard parametrization of \Ref\ \cite{Tanabashi:2018oca}.  The routines take $\sin\theta_{ij}$ rather than $\theta_{ij}$, which is the other convention worth stating: a value passed as an angle where a sine is expected is a silent error, not a loud one.  

To compute the probabilities in \figu{prob_3nu}, we fix the mixing parameters to their best-fit values provided by the recent NuFit 4.0 global fit to oscillation data\ \cite{Esteban:2018azc, NuFit4.0}, assuming normal mass hierarchy, and including Super-Kamiokande atmospheric neutrino data: $\sin^2 \theta_{12} = 0.310$, $\sin^2 \theta_{23} = 0.582$, $\sin^2 \theta_{13} = 0.02240$, $\delta_{\rm CP} = 217^\circ$, $\Delta m_{21}^2 = 7.39 \cdot 10^{-5}$~eV$^2$, $\Delta m_{31}^2 = 2.525 \cdot 10^{-3}$~eV$^2$.  Global fits have since moved on, but the default values carried by the code remain pinned to NuFit 4.0, so that the figures and examples in this paper stay reproducible.  Users are expected to supply current values for their own analyses.


\subsection{Oscillations in matter of constant density}
\label{section:prob_3nu_matter}

When neutrinos propagate in matter, $\nu_e$ and $\bar{\nu}_e$ scatter on electrons via charged-current interactions.  The interactions introduce potentials that shift the energies of the neutrinos.  As a result, the values of the mass-squared differences and mixing angles in matter differ from their values in vacuum, and depend on the number density of electrons\ \cite{Wolfenstein:1977ue, Mikheev:1986gs, Bethe:1986ej, Parke:1986jy, Rosen:1986jy}.  Computing oscillation probabilities in constant matter is integral to long-baseline experiments, where neutrinos traverse hundreds of kilometers in the crust of the Earth to reach the detectors\ \cite{Feldman:2013vca, Diwan:2016gmz}.

The Hamiltonian that drives oscillations in matter is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_matt}
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm matt}(E)
 = 
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm vac}(E) + \mathbb{A}_3 \;.
\end{equation}
The term $\mathbb{A}_3 \equiv {\rm diag}(V_{\rm CC}, 0 ,0)$ is due to interactions with matter, where $V_{\rm CC} = \sqrt{2} G_{\rm F} n_e$ is the charged-current potential and $n_e$ is the number density of electrons.

To compute the probabilities for oscillations in matter (and also with non-standard interactions) in \figu{prob_3nu}, we consider a constant matter density of $\rho = 3$~g~cm$^{-3}$, the average density of the crust of the Earth\ \cite{Dziewonski:1981xy}.  The number density of electrons is $n_e = Y_e \rho / [(m_p + m_n)/2]$, where $m_p$ and $m_n$ are the masses of the proton and neutron, respectively, and $Y_e = 0.5$ is the average electron fraction in the crust, which is electrically neutral.\footnote{Dividing by the mean free-nucleon mass, rather than by the atomic mass unit, neglects the binding energy of nucleons inside nuclei.  This undercounts $n_e$, and hence $V_{\rm CC}$, by about $0.8\%$.  The code does the same, so that it matches the expression above; the effect is far below the precision at issue in the examples here, but it is worth knowing about before the code is used where that level of accuracy matters.}  See \Refs\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb, Ohlsson:1999um} for the analytic form of the probabilities in matter, deduced with the OS method, and \Ref\ \cite{Freund:2001pn} for a related approximation.  In long-baseline experiments, even if there are density changes along the trajectory of the neutrino beam, using the average density is a good approximation\ \cite{Petcov:1998su, Freund:1999gy}.  

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\begin{figure}[t!]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{slabs_composition.pdf}
 \caption{\label{fig:slabs}Composition of the evolution operators for a neutrino crossing slabs of matter of constant but different density.  A neutrino enters as $\nu_\alpha$ on the left and leaves as $\nu_\beta$ on the right.  Each slab $k$ has its own width $L_k$, density $\rho_k$, and Hamiltonian $\mathbb{H}_k$, and is solved exactly to give its own evolution operator $\mathbb{U}_k(L_k)$.  The operators are then multiplied.  Note the order, indicated by the crossing dashed lines: because the operators act to the left on the initial state, the slab crossed \emph{first} is applied first and so appears \emph{rightmost} in the product, $\mathbb{U} = \mathbb{U}_4(L_4) \, \mathbb{U}_3(L_3) \, \mathbb{U}_2(L_2) \, \mathbb{U}_1(L_1)$.  Reversing the order describes a neutrino crossing the same profile in the opposite direction, and gives different probabilities.  See \equ{slabs_product}.}
\end{figure}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The result above can be extended to the case where neutrinos traverse multiple slabs of matter, each of constant, different density.  See, \eg, \Refs\ \cite{Chizhov:1999az, Chizhov:1999he}, for an overview of this scenario, and \Refs\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999um, Ohlsson:2001et, Merfeld:2014cha} for studies with the OS method.  This applies to long-baseline neutrino experiments that consider a non-uniform matter density profile\ \cite{Jacobsson:2001zk, Ohlsson:2003ip, Arguelles:2012nw, Roe:2017zdw, Kelly:2018kmb}, and to Earth-traversing neutrinos that cross multiple density layers inside Earth\ \cite{Freund:1999vc}.  The probability amplitudes obtained after traversing each slab need to be stitched together\ \cite{Giunti:2007ry}.  If a neutrino created as $\nu_\alpha$ traverses $N_{\rm slabs}$ slabs of constant-density matter, each of width $L_j$, then the evolved state is


\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:slabs_product}
 \nu_\alpha\left(\left\{L_j\right\}\right)
 =
 \left[
  \mathbb{U}_3^{\left(N_{\rm slabs}\right)}\left(L_{N_{\rm slabs}}\right) \cdots
  \mathbb{U}_3^{\left(2\right)}\left(L_2\right) \,
  \mathbb{U}_3^{\left(1\right)}\left(L_1\right)
 \right]
 \nu_\alpha \;,
\end{equation}

where $\mathbb{U}_3^{(j)}$ is \equ{u3_expanded} computed using the matter Hamiltonian evaluated with the matter density of the $j$-th slab.  The order of the factors matters, and is fixed by the fact that the operators act to the left on the initial state: the slab that the neutrino crosses \emph{first} is applied first and therefore stands \emph{rightmost} in the product.  Because the $\mathbb{U}_3^{(j)}$ do not commute in general, writing the product in the opposite order corresponds to sending the neutrino through the profile backwards, and gives a different answer.  This is the most common mistake made when implementing the procedure by hand.  The final oscillation probability is $P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta}\left(\left\{ L_j \right\}\right) = \lvert \nu_\beta^\dagger \nu_\alpha\left(\left\{L_j\right\}\right) \rvert^2$.

Figure \ref{fig:slabs} shows the procedure in full: a neutrino entering a sequence of slabs of differing width and density, the Hamiltonian and the exactly-solved evolution operator belonging to each, and the order in which those operators are multiplied.  It is implemented in the {\tt slabs} module, which takes arbitrary lists of widths and densities, solves each slab exactly, and composes the resulting operators in the order above.  The {\tt earth} module builds on it, using the PREM density profile\ \cite{Dziewonski:1981xy} to generate the slabs crossed along any chord through Earth.


\subsection{Oscillations with non-standard interactions}

Oscillations in matter might receive sub-leading contributions due to new neutrino interactions with the fermions of the medium that they propagate in.  These are known as non-standard interactions (NSI); see \Refs\ \cite{Ohlsson:2012kf, Miranda:2015dra, Farzan:2017xzy, Esteban:2018ppq} for reviews.  

In this case, the Hamiltonian is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_nsi}
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm NSI}(E)
 =
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm vac}(E) + \mathbb{A}_3 + \mathbb{V}_3  \;,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{V}_3 \equiv V_{\rm CC} \epsilon_3$ is the matter potential due to NSI and $\epsilon_3$ is the matrix of NSI strength parameters, \ie,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:epsilon_3}
 \epsilon_3
 =
 \left(
  \begin{array}{ccc}
   \epsilon_{ee}         & \epsilon_{e\mu}         & \epsilon_{e\tau} \\
   \epsilon_{e\mu}^\ast  & \epsilon_{\mu\mu}       & \epsilon_{\mu\tau} \\
   \epsilon_{e\tau}^\ast & \epsilon_{\mu\tau}^\ast & \epsilon_{\tau\tau} \\
  \end{array}
 \right) \;.
\end{equation}
The parameters $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}$ represent the total strength of the NSI between leptons of flavors $\alpha$ and $\beta$ interacting with the electrons, $u$ quarks, and $d$ quarks that make up standard matter.  Following \Ref\ \cite{Esteban:2018ppq}, we write $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta} = \epsilon_{\alpha\beta}^e + (2+ Y_n)\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}^u + (1+2Y_n)\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}^d$, with the ratio of the number densities of neutrons to electrons $Y_n \equiv n_n/n_e \approx 1$ in the Earth.  In our simplified treatment, we do not consider separately interactions with each fermion type or each chiral projection of the fermion\ \cite{Ohlsson:2012kf, Miranda:2015dra, Farzan:2017xzy, Esteban:2018ppq}.  

To compute the probabilities for NSI in \figu{prob_3nu}, we again consider propagation in the constant-density crust of the Earth, with $V_{\rm CC}$ evaluated as in Section \ref{section:prob_3nu_matter}.  Because NSI have not been observed, we choose illustrative values for the strength parameters, of the order of those explored in global fits to oscillation plus COHERENT data\ \cite{Esteban:2018ppq} (see also \Refs\ \cite{Mitsuka:2011ty, Esmaili:2013fva, Salvado:2016uqu, Aartsen:2017xtt}): $\epsilon_{ee}^u = -\epsilon_{e\mu}^u = 0.01$, $\epsilon_{\mu\mu}^u = 0.2$, $\epsilon_{e\tau}^u = \epsilon_{\mu\tau}^u = \epsilon_{\tau\tau}^u = 0$, and the same for $d$ quarks.  Like in \Ref\ \cite{Esteban:2018ppq}, we set all $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}^e = 0$.  Thus, for \figu{prob_3nu}, the NSI parameters in \equ{epsilon_3} are $\epsilon_{ee} = -\epsilon_{e\mu} = 0.06$, $\epsilon_{\mu\mu} = 1.2$, and $\epsilon_{e\tau} = \epsilon_{\mu\tau} = \epsilon_{\tau\tau} = 0$.

These values are illustrative, and should not be read as a fit.  The combination that matter oscillations are chiefly sensitive to is $\epsilon_{ee} - \epsilon_{\mu\mu} = -1.14$, which lies in the LMA-D, or ``dark-side,'' region rather than in the ordinary LMA one\ \cite{Esteban:2018ppq}.  We keep the values, because they are deliberately large: their purpose is to make the NSI curves in \figu{prob_3nu} visibly different from the standard ones, not to represent a fit.  They should not be reused as though they were one.


\subsection{Oscillations in a Lorentz-violating background}

Lorentz invariance is one of the linchpins of the Standard Model (SM), but is violated in proposed extensions, some related to quantum gravity; see \Refs\ \cite{Mavromatos:2004sz, Liberati:2009pf, Liberati:2013xla, Tasson:2014dfa} for reviews.  There is no experimental evidence for Lorentz-invariance violation (LIV), but there are stringent constraints on it\ \cite{Kostelecky:2008ts, Abbasi:2010kx, Abe:2014wla, Aartsen:2017ibm}.  The effects of LIV are numerous, \eg, changes in the properties and rates of processes of particles versus their anti-particles, introduction of anisotropies in particle angular distributions, and, in the case of neutrinos, changes to the effective mixing parameters and, thus, to the oscillation probabilities.

To study LIV, we adopt the framework of the Standard Model Extension (SME)\ \cite{Colladay:1998fq}, an effective field theory that augments the SM by adding LIV parameters to all sectors, including neutrinos\ \cite{Coleman:1998ti, Kostelecky:2003xn, Kostelecky:2003cr,  GonzalezGarcia:2004wg, Hooper:2005jp, Kostelecky:2011gq, Diaz:2014yva}.  In the SME, LIV is suppressed by a high energy scale $\Lambda$, still undetermined.  We focus on CPT-odd LIV, where the CPT symmetry is also broken.  This is realized by means of a new vector coupling of neutrinos to a new LIV background field.  Unlike the other oscillation cases presented above, the contribution of CPT-odd LIV to the Hamiltonian grows with neutrino energy.  This makes high-energy atmospheric and astrophysical neutrinos ideal for testing LIV\ \cite{Coleman:1998ti, GonzalezGarcia:2004wg, GonzalezGarcia:2005xw, Anchordoqui:2005gj, Arguelles:2015dca, Bustamante:2015waa, Rasmussen:2017ert, Aartsen:2017ibm, Ahlers:2018mkf, Ackermann:2019cxh}.

The Hamiltonian for CPT-odd LIV is\ \cite{Coleman:1998ti, Kostelecky:2003xn, Dighe:2008bu}
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h3_liv}
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm LIV}(E)
 = 
 \mathbb{H}_3^{\rm vac}(E)
 + 
 \frac{E}{\Lambda} \mathbb{R}_{3,\xi} \mathbb{B}_3 \mathbb{R}_{3,\xi}^\dagger \;.
\end{equation}
The second term on the right-hand side is the effective Hamiltonian that introduces LIV.  Here, $\mathbb{B}_3 \equiv {\rm diag}(b_1, b_2, b_3)$, where $b_i$ ($i=1,2,3$) are the eigenvalues of the LIV operator $\mathbb{B}_3$, and $\mathbb{R}_{3,\xi}$ is the $3 \times 3$ mixing matrix that rotates it into the flavor basis.  It has the same structure as the PMNS matrix, but different values of the mixing angles and phase.  In general, there is a relative phase between $\mathbb{R}_{3,\xi}$ and $\mathbb{R}_{3,\theta}$ that cannot be rotated away\ \cite{GonzalezGarcia:2004wg, GonzalezGarcia:2005xw}; in our simplified treatment, we set it to zero.

import earth
import slabs

        D21_NO_BF, D31_NO_BF)

        h_vac, energy,
        earth.matter_potential(densities))

# The electrons of the Earth source a Yukawa potential
# v_emu [eV], evaluated at the radius of each slab, that

Because LIV has not been observed, the values of the eigenvalues $b_i$ and of the LIV mixing parameters $\xi$ are undetermined.  Current upper limits\ \cite{Aartsen:2017ibm} set using high-energy atmospheric neutrinos imply that $b_i/\Lambda \lesssim 10^{-28}$ (this is $\mathring{c}^{(4)}$ in the notation of \Ref\ \cite{Aartsen:2017ibm}).  The LIV energy scale is believed to be at least $\Lambda = 1$~TeV.  However, to compute the probabilities for LIV in \figu{prob_3nu} such that they exhibit features at the lower energies used in the plot, we set artificially high values: $b_1/\Lambda = b_2/\Lambda = 10^{-21}$ and $b_3/\Lambda = 2 \cdot 10^{-21}$.  For simplicity, we set all mixing angles to zero, so that $\mathbb{R}_{3,\xi} = \mathbb{1}$.  This is the value carried by the {\tt globaldefs} module, and the one with which both \figu{prob_3nu} and the output printed in Listing\ \ref{lst:3nu_examples} were produced.

\subsection{Oscillations with arbitrary Hamiltonians}

The usefulness of {\tt NuOscProbExact} stems in part from its ability to compute oscillation probabilities for any arbitrary time-independent Hamiltonian.   Listing \ref{lst:3nu_arbitrary} shows a code template to compute three-neutrino oscillation probabilities using a user-supplied Hamiltonian that is added to the Hamiltonian for oscillations in matter.

The template is written around a concrete instance rather than a placeholder, because the placeholder hides what is being claimed.  The instance is a long-range interaction: a new neutral gauge boson $Z^\prime$ coupled to the anomaly-free combination $L_e - L_\mu$\ \cite{He:1991qd, Foot:1994vd}, under which the $\sim\!10^{51}$ electrons of the Earth source a Yukawa potential $V_{e\mu}(r)$ that $\nu_e$ and $\nu_\mu$ feel with opposite sign.  What makes it a useful example is that it is not merely a new number in an existing formula.  The potential is \emph{non-local}: at mediator masses light enough to matter, $m_{Z^\prime} \lesssim 1/R_\oplus$, its value at any radius is an integral over the whole electron distribution, so it depends on how many electrons there are and only weakly on where they sit\ \cite{Wise:2018rnb}.  It therefore cannot be absorbed into an effective density profile, and no specialized expression for it exists in any of the codes of Section \ref{section:comparison}.  The scenario and its phenomenology are developed in \Refs\ \cite{Joshipura:2003jj, Bandyopadhyay:2006uh, Bustamante:2018mzu, Agarwalla:2024lri}; here it is some thirty lines, and the probability that comes out is exact.

\figu{lri_earth} shows the result for a neutrino crossing the full diameter of the Earth.  The two curves are the same PREM slab decomposition, evaluated with and without the extra term, and the only difference between the calculations that produced them is the single line that adds $V_{e\mu}$ to the matter Hamiltonian.  At $\alpha^\prime \equiv g^{\prime 2}/4\pi = 10^{-52}$ --- a coupling at which $V_{e\mu}$ at the center of the Earth is about $1\%$ of $V_{\rm CC}$ there --- the appearance probability is displaced by up to $0.10$ in absolute terms, with the largest effect near $4$~GeV, where the standard matter resonance sits.  The lower panel is that difference on its own scale.  The point of the figure is not the parameter value, which is chosen to make the effect visible rather than to bound anything; it is that a scenario none of the codes of Section \ref{section:comparison} anticipates costs the user one array and one addition.  Notebook 20 in the repository builds $V_{e\mu}$ from scratch, checks it against its short- and long-range limits, and scans $\alpha^\prime$ over three decades.

\section{Performance and precision}
\label{section:performance}

Every timing quoted in this paper --- in this section, in \figu{plane}, in \figu{prem_plane} and in \figu{prem_plane_3plus1} --- was measured on one laptop: an Intel Core i5-1334U, ten cores and twelve threads at up to $4.6$~GHz, with $16$~GB of memory, running Ubuntu $24.04.4$ LTS with {\tt Python}~$3.12.7$, {\tt numpy}~$1.26.4$, {\tt numba}~$0.60.0$ and {\tt gcc}~$13.3$.  The external codes were compiled on the same machine with the optimisation flags their own documentation recommends.  Absolute times therefore mean little on their own and would be perhaps a factor of two different on a workstation; the ratios between codes, which is what these figures are for, are far more stable than that.

The closed forms are cheap, but how cheap depends almost entirely on how they are called.  \figu{performance} shows the cost of a single three-flavor probability as a function of the number requested at once.

Called one point at a time, the cost per probability is flat, at some tens of microseconds, and is dominated by Python rather than by arithmetic.  Passing a stack of Hamiltonians instead --- which every core routine accepts, together with an array of baselines, broadcast against each other --- amortizes that overhead over the whole stack, and by a few thousand points is roughly $20$ to $30$ times faster per probability.  This is the difference between an oscillogram taking minutes and taking seconds, and it is why the figures of Section \ref{section:code} are each a single call.  Routing the same stack through the optional compiled kernel gains a further factor of about $4$ to $5$.  The kernel is used only where it has been measured to win: at three and four flavors that is any stack at all, at two flavors only stacks of some tens of thousands, below which the {\tt numpy} path is faster than the cost of dispatching to the kernel.

\figu{performance} also carries two independent codes, and the four orders of magnitude that separate them are structural rather than incidental.  {\tt NuFast-LBL} is the fastest, at some $40$~ns per probability: it evaluates a closed form specialized to three flavors in constant matter, in compiled C++, with no Python in the loop at all and with the eigenvalues obtained from an approximation rather than from a cubic.  This code, batched and through the compiled kernel, is within a factor of two of that, which is as close as a general construction can reasonably come to a specialized one.  {\tt nuSQuIDS} is some $300~\mu$s per probability, four orders of magnitude slower, and for a reason that is not a deficiency: it integrates a differential equation for each energy, with adaptive stepping, rather than evaluating any closed form.  That is what buys the interactions and open-system terms a closed form cannot express, and it is the same property that produced its $10^{-8}$ integration floor in Section \ref{section:differences}.  The comparison in \figu{performance} is therefore between different kinds of calculation, not between better and worse implementations of one; the honest reading is that each code is fast at the problem it was built for.

Memory is the constraint that replaces time once the stacks are large.  A batched call holds three things at once: the stack of Hamiltonians, the result, and its own working space.  At three flavors these are $144$, $72$, and about $80$ bytes per energy, so a scan of $10^6$ points needs some $300$~MB and one of $10^7$ would not fit comfortably on a laptop.  The remedy is not to abandon batching but to batch in chunks: the speed in \figu{performance} is already saturated by a few thousand points, so nothing is lost by evaluating a long scan in blocks of, say, $10^5$ and accumulating the result.  The four-flavor case scales as $n^2$ in the Hamiltonian and $n^2$ in the output, so its stacks are roughly twice as large.

Three habits follow from all of this, and they are most of what a user needs to know.  Pass arrays rather than looping, since that is worth more than any other single choice.  Build the energy-independent part of the vacuum Hamiltonian once and reuse it --- every routine in {\tt hamiltonians3nu} is written to accept it --- rather than rebuilding it inside a scan.  And choose the slab count deliberately when crossing Earth: the cost is linear in the number of slabs, and resolving the PREM layers is worth doing, but subdividing far beyond the point where the probability stops changing buys nothing.

Precision is the other half of the question, and it does not trade against speed here: the three paths agree with one another to round-off, and with an independent code to the tolerance of its solver, as \figu{validation} and \figu{exact_vs_approx} show.  What precision does trade against is the spectrum.  Forming the characteristic polynomial and then extracting its roots costs a power of the relative spectral gap, so that near a level crossing --- where resonances live, and where the physics is most interesting --- the closed form loses accuracy that a backward-stable eigensolver does not.  Section \ref{section:code} states this among the limitations, and it is the one place where a user who needs the last digits should prefer diagonalization to the expressions given here.

Several mature codes compute oscillation probabilities, and they overlap less than their common subject suggests.  {\tt GLoBES}\ \cite{GLoBES} is an experiment-simulation framework, in which probabilities are one layer inside a package whose purpose is fluxes, cross-sections, detector response, and the statistics built on them.  {\tt Prob3++}\ \cite{Prob3pp} evaluates the three-flavor probability in matter through the analytic treatment of \Ref\ \cite{Barger:1980tf}, and is long established inside atmospheric-neutrino analyses.  {\tt nuCraft}\ \cite{nuCraft} integrates the evolution numerically along a trajectory through Earth, with sterile states allowed.  {\tt NuFast-LBL}\ \cite{NuFastLBL} solves the three-flavor constant-density case specifically, and does it faster than anything else available.  {\tt nuSQuIDS}\ \cite{Delgado:2014kpa, NuSQuIDS} is the most general: it propagates a density matrix rather than a state, which admits non-coherent interactions, attenuation, and open-system terms that no closed form can reach, at the cost of solving a differential equation.

Against that, what is distinctive here is narrow, and worth stating plainly.  This code takes an \emph{arbitrary} Hermitian Hamiltonian at two, three, or four flavors and returns the exact probability, with neither approximation nor numerical integration.  Nothing about the scenario needs to be anticipated: non-standard interactions, Lorentz violation, sterile states, and combinations of them are the same call, and a Hamiltonian nobody has yet written down is also the same call.  The price of that generality is that it exploits no structure, and for the standard three-flavor case --- where structure is exactly what {\tt NuFast-LBL} exploits --- it is slower and no more accurate in any way that matters.

\subsection{Where the differences come from}
\label{section:differences}

\figu{exact_vs_approx} makes that comparison quantitative, and the sizes of the residuals are worth accounting for individually, because none of them is what it first appears.

Before anything can be compared, the matter potential has to be made common.  This code takes the electron density as $Y_e \rho / \bar{m}$, with $\bar{m}$ the mean free-nucleon mass; both {\tt nuSQuIDS} and {\tt NuFast-LBL} take $\rho N_A Y_e$.  The two differ by the nuclear mass defect, $0.79\%$, which is far larger than any effect discussed below, so the density handed to the other two codes is rescaled by that factor.  What remains is then physics and arithmetic rather than bookkeeping.

Against {\tt NuFast-LBL} at its default setting the difference is $1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$, and it is a truncation, not an error.  That code obtains the eigenvalues of the matter Hamiltonian from a fast approximation refined by Newton's method, and its default performs no refinement at all.  Asking for two Newton steps drops the difference to $2.2 \cdot 10^{-8}$, which is the second effect: the two codes do not share a value of $\hbar c$.  {\tt NuFast-LBL} converts kilometres with $\hbar c = 1.97327 \cdot 10^{-7}$~eV~m, this code with a six-figure constant, and the two disagree by $4 \cdot 10^{-8}$ in relative terms.  Adopting their constant collapses the residual to $4 \cdot 10^{-12}$ --- round-off.  So the entire disagreement is one truncation the user controls and one convention neither code is wrong about.

Against {\tt nuSQuIDS} the difference is $8.9 \cdot 10^{-8}$, and the same length convention accounts for most of it: their kilometre differs from ours by $1.4 \cdot 10^{-7}$ relative, and adopting it leaves $1.9 \cdot 10^{-8}$.  That remainder is the numerical integration.  Three observations fix it as such.  In vacuum the two codes agree to $5 \cdot 10^{-16}$, because there {\tt nuSQuIDS} has nothing to integrate --- its interaction picture makes the vacuum evolution analytic.  In matter, tightening its solver tolerance from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-12}$ improves the agreement from $8 \cdot 10^{-8}$ to $1.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$, and tightening further to $10^{-16}$ changes nothing, so the residual is a floor rather than a setting.  And varying the density about the matched value only makes the agreement worse, so it is not a leftover mismatch of potentials.  What is left is the accumulated round-off of integrating an ordinary differential equation over $1300$~km, which is the price {\tt nuSQuIDS} pays for being able to integrate things a closed form cannot express at all.

The analytic expansion is the one case where the difference is neither convention nor tolerance.  At $10^{-3}$ it is the truncation of the series itself, and it is not reducible: there is no setting to tighten.  At this baseline that is still far below the systematic uncertainty of any experiment, which is the honest reason the expansion remains the right tool for the job it was built for.

A comparison at a single setting can be arranged to favour whichever code one prefers, because most of these codes have a knob that trades accuracy for time.  \figu{plane} therefore plots the knob.

Read it plainly, and this code does not come out ahead.  {\tt NuFast-LBL} occupies the lower-left corner and is not close: at its default it is five times faster than the best configuration here and accurate to $1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$, and two Newton steps take it to $8 \cdot 10^{-12}$ for a further $40\%$ of its own cost.  {\tt GLoBES} is faster than this code and accurate to $10^{-7}$.  On the standard three-flavor problem in constant matter, both are simply better tools, and a reader whose problem is that problem should use them.

What \figu{plane} also shows is the shape of the difference.  Every other entry sits somewhere on a trade: {\tt nuSQuIDS} buys accuracy with integration time and stops improving at $2 \cdot 10^{-8}$, where its differential-equation solver reaches its own floor; {\tt NuFast-LBL} buys it with Newton steps; the analytic expansion cannot buy it at all, and sits at $6.5 \cdot 10^{-3}$.  {\tt NuOscProbExact} has no such axis.  It returns the exact answer to round-off in every configuration, and the only thing that moves it is how many probabilities are requested at once --- a factor of $140$ between one at a time and a batched call through the compiled kernel.

That is the honest statement of what this construction is for.  It is not the fastest route to a three-flavor probability in constant matter, and \figu{plane} says so.  Its value is that its position on the plot does not depend on the Hamiltonian: the same call, at the same accuracy, serves four flavors, sterile states, non-standard interactions, a Lorentz-violating background, or a Hamiltonian nobody has yet written down --- none of which the other entries in \figu{plane} can take at all, and for which the question of which is faster therefore does not arise.

\subsection{What each code is for}
\label{section:capabilities}

Table \ref{tab:codes} sets out what these codes can and cannot do, which is a different question from how fast and how accurate they are, and often the more decisive one.  Two divisions run through it.

The first is the number of flavors.  {\tt Prob3++}, {\tt NuFast-LBL} and {\tt NuFast-Earth} fix three in their interfaces --- {\tt Prob3++} in a $3 \times 3$ probability array, the two {\tt NuFast} codes in a parameter list of three angles and two splittings --- and {\tt GLoBES} does the same, although it can be extended through a documented hook.  A sterile state is therefore not a setting in four of the seven codes but a different program.  Of the three that do accept one, {\tt nuSQuIDS} and {\tt nuCraft} reach it by propagating numerically in a basis of whatever size is asked for, and the construction of Section \ref{section:four_flavors} reaches it in closed form.

The second is what may be put in the Hamiltonian.  Most of these codes take oscillation parameters --- angles, phases, mass splittings --- and build the Hamiltonian themselves, which is what makes them convenient and also what fixes the physics they can express.  {\tt nuSQuIDS} and the code described here instead take the Hamiltonian, which is why non-standard interactions, Lorentz violation, or a scenario not yet proposed are the same call rather than a modification of the source.

\subsection{What to use, and when}
\label{section:what_to_use}

The practical conclusion is short.  For a production three-flavor fit at a long baseline through roughly constant density --- the case that most of the field spends most of its time on --- {\tt NuFast-LBL} is faster than this code by a wide margin and accurate well beyond experimental need; there is no reason to prefer the expressions given here.  Near a level crossing, where a resonance makes the spectrum nearly degenerate, forming the characteristic polynomial and extracting its roots is the wrong thing to do, and a backward-stable eigensolver is right.  For an open system, or for interactions, no closed form applies and {\tt nuSQuIDS} is the only option.

What is left, and what this code is for, is the arbitrary Hamiltonian: four flavors, sterile states, non-standard interactions, Lorentz violation, or something not yet proposed, for which no tailored expression exists and none is likely to be derived --- and, separately, the case of wanting a great many probabilities at once, where \figu{performance} shows what batching is worth.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{section:conclusions}

We have provided the code {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact} to compute exact two-neutrino, three-neutrino, and four-neutrino oscillation probabilities for arbitrary time-independent Hamiltonians.  The code is a numerical implementation of the method developed by Ohlsson \& Snellman\ \cite{Barger:1980tf, Ohlsson:1999xb, Ohlsson:1999um, Ohlsson:2000zz} and uses exponential expansions of SU(2), SU(3), and SU(4) matrices to bypass the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian.  It can be used to compute oscillation probabilities in many often-studied oscillation scenarios, including, but not limited to, oscillations in vacuum, in constant matter density, non-standard neutrino interactions, and new-physics scenarios, like Lorentz-invariance violation.

Three features widen the range of application beyond the basic case.  It treats matter of piece-wise constant density by solving each slab exactly and composing the resulting evolution operators, and it applies this to the density profile of Earth, so that probabilities can be computed along any chord through it.  It evaluates stacks of Hamiltonians and arrays of baselines without a Python-level loop, which is one to two orders of magnitude faster than looping in Python and is what makes parameter scans and oscillograms practical.  And it extends the method to four flavors, which brings 3+1 sterile-neutrino systems within its scope.  The code is available from PyPI, with {\tt pip install nuoscprobexact}, as well as from its repository\ \cite{NuOscProbExact}.

In developing {\tt NuOscProbExact}, our goal was to provide a general-purpose numerical code to compute exact oscillation probabilities that is also lightweight and can be easily incorporated into diverse oscillation analyses of standard and non-standard oscillations.  This is especially useful in the case of three-neutrino oscillations, where analytic expressions of the probabilities are often unavailable.  The code is suitable for exploring wide parameter spaces where approximate expressions of the probabilities are not available.  We provide it with this application in mind.

\subsection{The SU(4) generators}
\label{appendix:matrices_4nu}

For $n=2$ and $n=3$ the numbering of the generators is fixed by convention.  For $n=4$ none exists, so we state ours; it is the one used in Table \ref{tab:prob_4nu} and in {\tt NuOscProbExact}.  Writing $E_{\alpha\beta}$ for the $4 \times 4$ matrix with a single $1$ in row $\alpha$ and column $\beta$, the fifteen generators are, in order,
\begin{equation*}
 \lambda^{2p-1} = E_{\alpha\beta} + E_{\beta\alpha} \;, \quad
 \lambda^{2p} = -i\left(E_{\alpha\beta} - E_{\beta\alpha}\right) \;,
\end{equation*}
for $p = 1, \ldots, 6$ running over the six flavor pairs $(\alpha,\beta)$ in lexicographic order --- $(e,\mu)$, $(e,\tau)$, $(e,s)$, $(\mu,\tau)$, $(\mu,s)$, $(\tau,s)$ --- followed by the three Cartan generators
\begin{equation*}
 \lambda^{12+l}
 =
 \sqrt{\frac{2}{l\left(l+1\right)}}\,
 {\rm diag}\big(\underbrace{1, \ldots, 1}_{l}, -l, 0, \ldots\big) \;,
 \quad
 l = 1, 2, 3 \;.
\end{equation*}
They satisfy ${\rm Tr}(\lambda^a\lambda^b) = 2\delta^{ab}$, as do the Pauli and Gell-Mann matrices.

Three properties motivate this choice.  Deleting the pairs that involve the fourth state reduces it to the standard Gell-Mann ordering on the upper-left $3 \times 3$ block, so that the three-flavor expressions embed unchanged; the two generators of a given pair are adjacent, so the $T$-conjugate structure of Table \ref{tab:prob_4nu} reads off consecutive indices; and the generator that singles out the sterile state, $\lambda^{15}$, comes last, so that adding a fourth state appends to the three-flavor labelling rather than renumbering it.

\section{Derivation of \equ{prob_2nu_general}}
\label{appendix:derivation_prob_2nu_general}

We proceed by computing the survival probability $P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\alpha} = \lvert \nu_\alpha^\dagger \mathbb{U}_2(L) \nu_\alpha \rvert^2$.  We start by operating on $\nu_\alpha$ with the linear combination $h_k \sigma^k$ that appears in the expansion of the time-evolution operator $\mathbb{U}_2(L)$, \equ{evol_op_2nu}, \ie, $(h_k \sigma^k) \nu_\alpha$.  In matrix form, with $\nu_\alpha = \left(1~0\right)^{\rm T}$, this is
\begin{equation*}
 \left(\begin{array}{cc}
  h_3      & h_1-ih_2 \\
  h_1+ih_2 & -h_3     \\
 \end{array}\right)
 \left(\begin{array}{c}
  1 \\
  0 \\
 \end{array}\right)
 =
 \left(\begin{array}{c}
  h_3      \\
  h_1+ih_2 \\
 \end{array}\right) \;.
\end{equation*}
So, using \equ{evol_op_2nu} for $\mathbb{U}_2(L)$, the survival probability amplitude is
\begin{equation*}
 \nu_\alpha^\dagger \mathbb{U}_2(L) \nu_\alpha
 =
 \cos\left(\left\vert h\right\vert L\right)
 -
 i \frac{h_3}{\lvert h \rvert} \sin\left(\left\vert h\right\vert L\right) \;,
\end{equation*}
where the coefficient $h_3 = [(\mathbb{H}_2)_{11} - (\mathbb{H}_2)_{22}]/2$; see Table \ref{tab:h2_coefficients}.  Since $\mathbb{H}_2$ is Hermitian, its diagonal elements are real, and, hence, $h_3$ is real.  Because of this, we can write the survival probability $\lvert \nu_\alpha^\dagger \mathbb{U}_2(L) \nu_\alpha \rvert^2$ as
\begin{equation*}
 P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\alpha}(L)
 =
 \cos^2(\lvert h \rvert L)
 +
 \frac{\lvert h_3 \rvert^2}{\lvert h \rvert^2} \sin^2(\lvert h \rvert L) \;.
\end{equation*}
Now, because $\vert h_3 \rvert^2 = \vert h \rvert^2 - \vert h_1 \rvert^2 - \vert h_2 \rvert^2$, this becomes
\begin{equation*}
 P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\alpha}(L)
 =
 1
 -
 \frac{\lvert h_1 \rvert^2 + \lvert h_2 \rvert^2}{\lvert h \rvert^2} \sin^2(\lvert h \rvert L) \;.
\end{equation*}
Because of the conservation of probability, $P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\beta} = 1 - P_{\nu_\alpha \to \nu_\alpha}$, with $\alpha \neq \beta$, which gives \equ{prob_2nu_general}.

\section{Two-flavor oscillations in vacuum}
\label{section:2nu_vacuum}

As a simple example and cross-check of the OS method, we consider two-flavor oscillations in vacuum.  These are driven by the mass-squared difference between two mass eigenstates $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$, with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, out of which the flavor states $\nu_e$ and $\nu_\mu$ are constructed (or $\nu_\mu$ and $\nu_\tau$).  The spaces of flavor and mass states are connected by a unitary rotation that is parametrized by a mixing angle $\theta$, \ie,
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:u_rotation_2nu}
 \mathbb{R}_{2,\theta}
 =
 \left(\begin{array}{cc}
  \cos \theta  & \sin \theta \\
  -\sin \theta & \cos \theta 
 \end{array}\right) \;.
\end{equation}
In this case, the Hamiltonian in the flavor basis, for a neutrino of energy $E$, is
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:h2_vacuum}
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm vac}(E)
 =
 \frac{1}{2E}
 \mathbb{R}_{2,\theta}
 \mathbb{M}_2^2
 \mathbb{R}_{2,\theta}^\dagger \;,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{M}_2^2~\equiv~$${\rm diag}(-\Delta m^2/2, \Delta m^2/2)$ is the mass matrix, and $\Delta m^2\equiv m_2^2-m_1^2$.

The larger mass-squared value sits on the \emph{second} mass eigenstate, which is the textbook traceless mass matrix and the ordering the code uses.  Placing it on the first gives the negative of that matrix, and it is worth being precise about what that does and does not affect.

The derivation that follows does not depend on which of the two is used.  The probability depends on the $h_k$ only through $\lvert h_1 \rvert^2 + \lvert h_2 \rvert^2$, $\lvert h \rvert^2$, and $\sin^2\left(\lvert h \rvert L\right)$, each of which is even under $h_k \to -h_k$.  Overall sign is therefore invisible in vacuum, and the standard two-flavor formula recovered below stands.

What the sign does affect is every Hamiltonian that \emph{adds} a term to $\mathbb{H}_2^{\rm vac}$: the matter, NSI, and LIV cases of \ref{appendix:sample_2nu_hamiltonians}.  Negating only the vacuum term reverses its sign relative to the matter potential, and the resulting probabilities are exactly those of the corresponding \emph{antineutrino} process --- for the LIV case, exactly those of the CPT-conjugate one, with $b_i \to -b_i$.  We have checked this identity numerically and it holds to machine precision.  The matter, NSI, and LIV panels of \figu{prob_2nu} are therefore computed with the ordering above, and would show the antineutrino probabilities under the other one; the vacuum panel does not depend on the choice.  Nothing in Section\ \ref{section:code} is affected, since this concerns the two-flavor Hamiltonian alone.

Using Table \ref{tab:h2_coefficients}, we identify
\begin{equation*}
 \lvert h_1 \rvert^2 = \frac{\Delta m^2}{2E} \sin^2\left(2\theta\right) \;, \;\;
 \lvert h_3 \rvert^2 = \frac{\Delta m^2}{2E} \cos^2\left(2\theta\right) \;,
\end{equation*}
and $\lvert h_2 \rvert^2 = 0$, so that $\lvert h_1 \rvert^2 / \lvert h \rvert^2  = \sin^2\left(2\theta\right)$.  From \equ{prob_2nu_general}, the probability is
\begin{equation*}
 P_{\nu_e \to \nu_\mu}^{\rm vac} (E, L)
 =
 \sin^2 \left(2\theta\right)
 \sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta m^2}{4E}L\right) \;,
\end{equation*}
which is the standard expression for two-neutrino oscillations in vacuum; see, \eg, \Refs\ \cite{Giunti:2007ry, Tanabashi:2018oca}.  
% See \Ref\ \cite{Ohlsson:1999xb} for a similar derivation to the one presented here.

\section{Derivation of \equ{uk_expanded}}
\label{appendix:derivation_uk_expanded}

First, we write 
\begin{equation*}
 u_k
 = 
 -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^3 e^{i\phi_m} \frac{\partial\phi_m}{\partial \left(-h_kL\right)} \;, 
\end{equation*}
where
\begin{equation*}
 \frac{\partial\phi_m}{\partial\left(-h_kL\right)}
 =
 \frac{2\left[\phi_m\left(-h_kL\right)+d_{ijk}\left(-h^iL\right)\left(-h^jL\right)\right]}{3\phi_m^2-I_2} \;.
\end{equation*}
This can be expanded as
\begin{eqnarray*}
 u_k 
 &=& -\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\frac{\partial}{\partial\left(-h_kL\right)}e^{-ih_k\lambda^kL}  \\
 &=& -\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\left(-h_kL\right)}\text{Tr}~\mathbb{U}_3 \\
 &=& -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=1}^3 e^{i\phi_m} \frac{\partial\phi_m}{\partial\left(-h_kL\right)} \;.
\end{eqnarray*}
Thus, the coefficients $u_k$ can be written as 
\begin{equation}
 \label{equ:uk_short}
 u_k = -L x h_k + L^2 y \left(h\ast h\right)_k \;,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{eqnarray}
 \label{equ:x_def}
 && x = - \frac{1}{L} \sum_{m=1}^3 \frac{ \psi_m e^{i L \psi_m}} { 3\psi_m^2-\lvert h \rvert^2 } \;, \\
 \label{equ:y_def}
 && y = - \frac{1}{L^2} \sum_{m=1}^3 \frac{ e^{i L \psi_m} } { 3\psi_m^2-\lvert h \rvert^2 } \;.
\end{eqnarray}
Inserting Eqs.\ (\ref{equ:x_def}) and (\ref{equ:y_def}) into \equ{uk_short} results in \equ{uk_expanded} in the main text.

\section{Sample two-neutrino Hamiltonians}
\label{appendix:sample_2nu_hamiltonians}

Here we present the two-neutrino Hamiltonians included as examples in {\tt NuOscProbExact}.  These are the two-neutrino counterparts of the three-neutrino examples presented in Section\ \ref{section:code}; we refer to that section for a description of each scenario.  All of the Hamiltonians below are written in the flavor basis.

Figure \ref{fig:prob_2nu} shows the probabilities $P_{\nu_e \to \nu_e}$, $P_{\nu_\mu \to \nu_e}$, and $P_{\nu_\mu \to \nu_\mu}$ for the same four scenarios as in \figu{prob_3nu}, computed using {\tt NuOscProbExact}\ \cite{NuOscProbExact}.  Again, we set the baseline to $L = 1300$~km.  The parameters and their values used in each example case are introduced below; they are a selection of the ones used in \figu{prob_3nu}.

For oscillations in vacuum, we use \equ{h2_vacuum}, \ie,
\begin{equation*}
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm vac}(E)
 =
 \frac{1}{2E}
 \mathbb{R}_{2,\theta}
 \mathbb{M}_2^2
 \mathbb{R}_{2,\theta}^\dagger \;,
\end{equation*}
where the rotation matrix $\mathbb{R}_{2,\theta}$ is given in \equ{u_rotation_2nu}, in terms of the mixing angle $\theta$.  In \figu{prob_2nu}, we set $\theta$ and $\Delta m^2$, respectively, to the values of $\theta_{23}$ and $\Delta m_{31}^2$ used in \figu{prob_3nu}.  These are also the values used by the two-neutrino examples bundled with the code, and they are the ones the figure requires: with $\Delta m_{21}^2$ the first minimum of $P_{\nu_e \to \nu_e}$ at $L = 1300$~km would fall at $E \simeq 0.08$~GeV, far below the range plotted, and its depth would be set by $\sin^2 2\theta_{12}$; the published figure instead shows that minimum at $E \simeq 2.6$~GeV with near-maximal depth, which is what $\Delta m_{31}^2$ and $\theta_{23}$ give.

For oscillations in matter, we use
\begin{equation*}
 \label{equ:h2_matt}
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm matt}(E) 
 = 
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm vac}(E)  + \mathbb{A}_2 \;,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbb{A}_2 \equiv {\rm diag}(V_{\rm CC}, 0)$ and $V_{\rm CC}$ is defined as before.  In \figu{prob_2nu}, we set $\rho = 3$~g~cm$^{-3}$.

For oscillations in matter with non-standard interactions, we use\ \cite{GonzalezGarcia:2004wg, GonzalezGarcia:2005xw}
\begin{equation*}
 \label{equ:h2_nsi}
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm NSI}(E) 
 = 
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm vac}(E) + \mathbb{A}_2 + \mathbb{V}_2 \;,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbb{V}_2 \equiv V_{\rm CC} \epsilon_2$ and the matrix of NSI strength parameters is
\begin{equation*}
 \epsilon_2
 =
 \left(
  \begin{array}{ccc}
   \epsilon_{ee}         & \epsilon_{e\mu}         \\
   \epsilon_{e\mu}^\ast  & \epsilon_{\mu\mu}       \\
  \end{array}
 \right) \;.
\end{equation*}
In \figu{prob_2nu}, we set $\epsilon_{ee} = -\epsilon_{e\mu} = 0.06$ and $\epsilon_{\mu\mu} = 1.2$.

For oscillations in a CPT-odd Lorentz-violating background, we use\ \cite{GonzalezGarcia:2004wg, GonzalezGarcia:2005xw} 
\begin{equation*}
 \label{equ:h2_liv}
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm LIV}(E) 
 = 
 \mathbb{H}_2^{\rm vac}(E) + \frac{E}{\Lambda} \mathbb{R}_{2,\xi} \mathbb{B}_2 \mathbb{R}_{2,\xi}^\dagger \;,
\end{equation*}
where $\mathbb{R}_{2,\xi}$ is a $2 \times 2$ rotation matrix, like \equ{u_rotation_2nu}, but evaluated at a different mixing angle $\xi$.  In \figu{prob_2nu}, we set $\mathbb{R}_{2,\xi} = \mathbb{1}$, $b_1/\Lambda = 10^{-21}$, and $b_2/\Lambda = 2 \cdot 10^{-21}$.  These are the values in {\tt globaldefs}, and the ones with which \figu{prob_2nu} was drawn; they match those used for \figu{prob_3nu} in Section\ \ref{section:code}.

\section{Speed against accuracy through the Earth}
\label{appendix:prem_plane}

\figu{plane} measures how exactly a code evaluates the probability for a Hamiltonian it is given.  Through the Earth that question cannot be asked, because no code is given the Hamiltonian: the density varies continuously along the trajectory, and every code, this one included, replaces the profile with something it can propagate through.  Figures\ \ref{fig:prem_plane} and\ \ref{fig:prem_plane_3plus1} therefore measure something else, and it is worth being explicit about what.

The reference is a \emph{converged} PREM solution rather than an exact one.  The slab product of \equ{slabs_product} converges at second order in the slab width --- the density is sampled at slab midpoints and the PREM shell boundaries are cut exactly, so the leading error is $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$, with measured exponents of $2.000$ at $3$, $10$, and $40$~GeV.  The reference is the second-order Richardson extrapolation $\left[4 P(256) - P(128)\right]/3$ of a $30$-digit slab product.  That extrapolation shares its discretisation with the code it is meant to judge, so it is itself refereed, by an adaptive integration of $d\psi/dx = -i \mathbb{H}(x) \psi$ through the continuous profile, restarted at every shell boundary so that no step straddles a discontinuity.  The two share nothing but the definition of PREM, and they agree to $2 \cdot 10^{-11}$ at three flavors and $1 \cdot 10^{-9}$ at $3+1$ --- four decades below the finest point plotted.

The second consequence is that {\tt NuOscProbExact} is a curve here and not the horizontal line of \figu{plane}, and it is worth being clear about why the tolerances exist at all.  In vacuum, and in matter of constant density, this code has no tolerance and needs none: the evolution operator is a closed-form function of the Hamiltonian, evaluated once, and the answer is exact to round-off --- which is what \figu{plane} shows, and why {\tt rtol} and {\tt atol} are absent from every example in Section\ \ref{section:code}.  The same is true of a chord through layered matter of piece-wise constant density: each slab is solved exactly and the operators are composed exactly, so nothing is approximated there either.  What PREM adds is that the density inside a segment is \emph{not} constant.  The profile has to be replaced by slabs that are, and only then does a discretisation exist to be traded against time.  Through the Earth it has a precision setting like everything else, and in fact two: the number of slabs per chord segment, and a relative tolerance from which the routine chooses that number itself, doubling until the answer settles.  Both are drawn.  The tolerance is the one to read first, because it is the only dial on any of these curves where the user states the quantity the vertical axis measures; a slab count is a number whose accuracy consequence one cannot know in advance, which is why the documentation's advice is to raise it and watch the result settle --- exactly what the tolerance automates.  The price of that convenience is visible as the gap between the two red curves, and it is about a factor of two: reaching $6.3 \cdot 10^{-7}$ costs $134$~$\mu$s when the code is told to use $256$ slabs and $252$~$\mu$s when it is asked to find that number, because the search evaluates at $1, 2, 4, \ldots$ and keeps only the last.  What each code's position measures, either way, is how finely it slices or integrates the profile, not the accuracy of its probability formula.


Read plainly, \figu{prem_plane} says something this paper's earlier figures do not.  On the Earth problem, at three flavors, this code occupies the lower-left corner: it reaches $6.3 \cdot 10^{-7}$ --- the best accuracy of any code here --- in $119$~$\mu$s, where {\tt GLoBES} needs $637$~$\mu$s to reach $6.6 \cdot 10^{-7}$ and {\tt NuFast-Earth} $404$~$\mu$s to reach $2.1 \cdot 10^{-6}$.  At every accuracy the six codes share, it is the fastest of them.  That is not the ordering of \figu{plane}, where {\tt NuFast-Earth} is five times faster than the best configuration here on the constant-density problem, and the difference between the two figures is worth being exact about: it is not the algebra, which is the same algebra, but the fact that a chord is a \emph{stack}.  The Earth routines take an array of energies and hand the whole stack of per-slab Hamiltonians to one compiled kernel, so the cost of a probability falls as more of them are asked for at once, which is the same property \figu{performance} measures at constant density.  {\tt NuFast-Earth} is still the code to reach for at a single point: at its coarsest setting it returns a probability in a microsecond.

What the figure shows in the other direction is where each code stops.  {\tt Prob3++} flattens first, at $3 \cdot 10^{-4}$; that is not its profile treatment but its own arithmetic, and it is the same floor as the $4.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ it shows in \figu{plane} carried over a chord with $8.8$ times the oscillation phase.  {\tt nuSQuIDS} flattens at $1 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and {\tt nuCraft} at $2.5 \cdot 10^{-6}$, and neither floor is a tolerance the user can tighten: for {\tt nuSQuIDS} the value is identical for five different {\tt GSL} steppers, for any maximum step size, and for every tolerance below $10^{-9}$.  What holds the two integrators there is the density discontinuities at the PREM shell boundaries, which an adaptive stepper cannot see and a tabulated profile cannot place exactly.  Only the three codes that cut the profile at fixed shells --- this one, {\tt NuFast-Earth} and {\tt GLoBES} --- reach below $10^{-6}$, and they do it because the boundaries are cut rather than sampled.

It is worth separating the two reasons a curve flattens, because they are not the same failure.  {\tt nuSQuIDS} and {\tt nuCraft} flatten because they integrate through a profile they can only sample: neither can place a density discontinuity exactly, and the floor each reaches, $1 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and $2.5 \cdot 10^{-6}$, is close to what each returns in vacuum on the same chord, where there is no profile at all.  Tightening a tolerance cannot help with that.  {\tt Prob3++} flattens for an unrelated reason: its dial is a shell count, not a tolerance, and adding shells stops helping once its own arithmetic dominates.  Its floor of $3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ is the $5.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ it returns in vacuum, which is in turn the $4.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ of \figu{plane} carried over a chord with $8.8$ times the phase.  {\tt NuFast-Earth} and {\tt GLoBES}, dialled the same way, do not flatten at all within the range plotted, because their arithmetic has further to fall.  Note also what the leftmost point of each curve costs: at one slab per segment the chord is seventeen slabs long, too short for the kernel launch to amortise, so the coarsest settings measure fixed overhead rather than the slab product.

\figu{prem_plane_3plus1} repeats the exercise with a sterile state, and four of the six curves disappear.  {\tt NuFast-Earth}, {\tt GLoBES} and {\tt Prob3++} have no sterile mode to run, and {\tt nuCraft} is kept out for the reason its caption gives.  That is the point of showing the second panel: what remains is unchanged in shape.  The slab product does not care how large the Hamiltonian is, and the same dial buys the same second-order convergence at four flavors as at three, while {\tt nuSQuIDS} again floors near $10^{-6}$.

The four-flavor panel draws one curve for this library, and that is itself a result.  Both routes to the latent roots were measured --- the double-double route that forms the invariants in $32$-digit arithmetic, and the eigensolver route that calls {\tt numpy.linalg.eigvalsh} and takes one Newton step --- and there is nothing to choose between them here.  They agree \emph{bit for bit} at every slab count, because a root difference of $3.6 \cdot 10^{-17}$ against $3.9 \cdot 10^{-16}$ is invisible under a discretisation error that never falls below $8 \cdot 10^{-7}$; and their costs differ by a median of $0.4\%$, against a run-to-run spread that reaches a factor of $1.6$ on individual pairs.  The choice matters where the roots themselves are stiff, which through the Earth at $\Delta m^2_{41} = 1$~eV$^2$ they are not.  Both are frozen in the repository for a reader who wants to check that.

\tabl{codes} lists, alongside what each code can do, the best accuracy it reached in each of the two planes, since a speed-accuracy figure says nothing about whether a code can express the problem at all --- which, for four of the six here, is the question that decides it.

The six codes divide into two families in how they treat the profile, and that division is what \figu{prem_plane} is really showing.  {\tt NuOscProbExact}, {\tt NuFast-Earth}, {\tt GLoBES} and {\tt Prob3++} cut the chord into shells of constant density and solve each exactly, composing the resulting operators; what they approximate is the profile, never the propagation through it, and their accuracy improves without limit as the shells are refined.  {\tt nuSQuIDS} and {\tt nuCraft} instead integrate the equations of motion numerically along the trajectory, sampling the density as they go; they approximate the propagation itself, which is what lets them handle problems no closed form reaches --- interactions, attenuation, open systems --- and what stops them placing a density discontinuity exactly.  This code differs from the other three in its family only in where it cuts: at the PREM shell boundaries themselves, and at slab midpoints within them, which is what makes its error fall as $1/N_{\rm slabs}^2$ rather than more slowly.

The conventions behind \figu{prem_plane} are worth setting out, because none of them is optional.  Each external code is dialled over the same range its own interface offers: $N_{\rm shells}$ from $1$ to $256$ for {\tt NuFast-Earth}, {\tt GLoBES} and {\tt Prob3++}, solver tolerances from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-12}$ for {\tt nuSQuIDS} and {\tt nuCraft}.  Every code is given the same oscillation parameters --- the NuFit 4.0 values of Section\ \ref{section:code}, and at $3+1$ the $\Delta m^2_{41}$ and mixings of \figu{sterile_earth} --- each expressed in the convention that code expects, which for {\tt Prob3++} means $\Delta m^2_{32}$ rather than $\Delta m^2_{31}$.  All five are handed this library's PREM at $Y_e = 0.5$, the same $V_{\rm CC}$, and the same chord in eV$^{-1}$, so that what is compared is the propagation and not the Earth model.  And each is given whatever batched interface it has, so that none is looped from outside when its own interface would take the whole stack: {\tt nuSQuIDS} through its multiple-energy constructor, worth a factor of $1.4$ to $1.8$ over driving it one point at a time; {\tt NuFast-Earth} through {\tt Set\_Spectra}; and {\tt nuCraft} through the particle list its {\tt CalcWeights} takes, which turns out to be worth nothing because it loops internally.  {\tt GLoBES} and {\tt Prob3++} expose no such entry point and are looped, which is their interface and not a handicap imposed here.  {\tt NuOscProbExact} is version $1.13.0$, timed with the compiled {\tt numba} kernels that a plain {\tt pip install nuoscprobexact} now provides, and called as a user would call it, with one batched request for all twelve energies.

Both figures are drawn by the same notebook as the rest of the paper, from data frozen in the repository; the generator that produces it records, in full, the conventions that had to be matched first --- the production heights that lengthen the chord if left at their defaults, the two different values of $\hbar c$, the two different definitions of the electron density, and the profile tabulations that had to be replaced by this library's PREM before any of the five were propagating through the same Earth as this code.
